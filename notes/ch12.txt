## SUMMARY 12.1
In short: An object is in static equilibrium when it stays at rest: the net force on it is zero AND the net torque on it is zero, \(\sum\vec F = 0\) and \(\sum\vec\tau = 0\). In equilibrium the net torque is zero about every point, so you may put the pivot anywhere. Put it where an unknown force acts, and that force drops out of the torque equation. Gravity acts as if all the weight sits at the center of gravity, which is the center of mass when \(g\) is the same everywhere on the object. The recipe: draw a free body diagram, pick a pivot, write \(\sum F_x = 0\), \(\sum F_y = 0\), \(\sum\tau = 0\), and solve.
Watch out: Zero net force is not enough. Two equal and opposite forces that don't act along the same line (a couple) give zero net force but a torque that starts the object spinning.

## SUMMARY 12.2
In short: The same recipe solves seesaws, arms, ladders, hinges, and signs. A seesaw balances when \(r_1w_1 = r_2w_2\): a heavier person sits closer to the pivot. A muscle attached close to a joint has a short lever arm, so it must pull many times harder than the load in the hand. A ladder leaning on a smooth wall gets a sideways push from the wall, and floor friction must match that push; the higher a person climbs, the more friction is needed. Guess a direction for each unknown force; a negative answer just means it points the other way.
Watch out: A torque uses the lever arm, the perpendicular distance from the pivot to the line of the force, \(\tau = r_\perp F = rF\sin\theta\), not just the distance to where the force acts.

## SUMMARY 12.3
In short: Stress is force per area, \(\frac{F}{A}\), in pascals (1 Pa = 1 N/m²). Strain is the fractional change in size, like \(\frac{\Delta L}{L_0}\), and has no units. For small deformations stress = (elastic modulus) × strain. Stretching or squeezing along a length uses Young's modulus \(Y\), so \(\Delta L = \frac{FL_0}{AY}\). Squeezing from all sides uses the bulk modulus \(B\): \(\frac{\Delta V}{V_0} = -\frac{\Delta p}{B}\). Pushing sideways on one face uses the shear modulus \(S\). A larger modulus means a stiffer material. Steel has \(Y = 2.0\times10^{11}\) Pa; water has \(B = 2.2\times10^{9}\) Pa.
Watch out: A wire twice as long stretches twice as much under the same force, but a wire twice as thick (diameter) has four times the area, so it stretches only one fourth as much.

## SUMMARY 12.4
In short: A material is elastic if it returns to its original shape when the stress is removed. Up to the proportionality limit, stress is proportional to strain (the straight part of a stress strain diagram). Between the proportionality limit and the elastic limit it still springs back, but stress and strain are no longer proportional. Past the elastic limit the behavior is plastic: the object stays permanently stretched or bent. It breaks at the breaking point, at the end of the plastic region.
Watch out: Going past the proportionality limit does not by itself cause a permanent change. That starts only past the elastic limit.

## EQUATIONS 12.1
* First condition for equilibrium: \(\sum\vec F = 0\) | The vector sum of all external forces (N) on the object is zero, so its center of mass doesn't accelerate. In components, \(\sum F_x = 0\) and \(\sum F_y = 0\).
* Second condition for equilibrium: \(\sum\vec\tau = 0\) | The sum of all external torques (N·m) is zero, so the object doesn't start to turn. It holds about every pivot point, so pick the most convenient one.
Torque of a force: \(\tau = rF\sin\theta = r_\perp F\) | A force \(F\) (N) acting \(r\) (m) from the pivot at angle \(\theta\) to \(\vec r\). The lever arm \(r_\perp\) is the perpendicular distance to the force's line.
Center of gravity: \(x_\text{CG} = \dfrac{m_1x_1 + m_2x_2 + \cdots}{m_1 + m_2 + \cdots}\) | Where the whole weight seems to act, with masses \(m\) (kg) at positions \(x\) (m). With uniform \(g\) it is the center of mass.

## EQUATIONS 12.2
* Seesaw balance: \(r_1w_1 = r_2w_2\) | Weights \(w_1\) and \(w_2\) (N) at distances \(r_1\) and \(r_2\) (m) on opposite sides of the pivot balance when their torques match.
Friction holding a ladder: \(f_s \le \mu_sN\) | Static friction \(f_s\) (N) at the floor can be at most \(\mu_s\) times the floor's normal force \(N\) (N). The ladder slips if it needs more.

## EQUATIONS 12.3
Stress: \(\text{stress} = \dfrac{F}{A}\) | Force \(F\) (N) spread over area \(A\) (m²), in pascals (Pa). Use the area perpendicular to the force for tension or compression.
* Stress and strain: \(\text{stress} = (\text{elastic modulus})\times\text{strain}\) | Holds for small deformations. Strain is a fraction with no units, so the modulus has the same units as stress (Pa).
* Young's modulus: \(Y = \dfrac{F_\perp}{A}\,\dfrac{L_0}{\Delta L}\) | A rod of length \(L_0\) (m) and cross section \(A\) (m²) pulled or pushed along its length by \(F_\perp\) (N) changes length by \(\Delta L\) (m).
Tensile strain: \(\text{strain} = \dfrac{\Delta L}{L_0}\) | The change in length divided by the original length. No units.
* Bulk modulus: \(B = -\Delta p\,\dfrac{V_0}{\Delta V}\) | A pressure increase \(\Delta p\) (Pa) on all sides shrinks volume \(V_0\) by \(\Delta V\) (m³). The minus sign makes \(B\) positive, since \(\Delta V\) is negative.
Compressibility: \(k = \dfrac{1}{B}\) | The inverse of the bulk modulus (1/Pa). A large compressibility means the material is easy to squeeze.
* Shear modulus: \(S = \dfrac{F_\parallel}{A}\,\dfrac{L_0}{\Delta x}\) | A sideways force \(F_\parallel\) (N) on a face of area \(A\) (m²) shifts that face by \(\Delta x\) (m) relative to the opposite face, a distance \(L_0\) (m) away.

## EQUATIONS 12.4
Below the proportionality limit: \(\text{stress} = Y\times\text{strain}\) | On the straight part of a stress strain diagram, the slope is Young's modulus \(Y\) (Pa). Past the proportionality limit the curve bends.

# ============================ 12.1 CONDITIONS FOR STATIC EQUILIBRIUM ============================

## QUIZ 12.1
Q: An object is in static equilibrium. Which must be true?
A) The net force and the net torque on it are both zero
B) Only the net force on it is zero
C) Only the net torque on it is zero
D) No forces act on it
Answer: A
Why: Zero net force keeps the center of mass from accelerating, and zero net torque keeps the object from starting to turn. Static equilibrium needs both. Forces can act; they just have to balance.

## QUIZ 12.1
Q: A meter stick lies on a frictionless table. Two forces of the same size push on its two ends, at right angles to the stick and in opposite directions. Is the stick in equilibrium?
A) No, the torques add and it starts to spin
B) Yes, the forces cancel
C) Yes, as long as the stick is light
D) No, the net force isn't zero
Answer: A
Why: The net force is zero, but the forces don't act along the same line. They form a couple, and both turn the stick the same way.

## QUIZ 12.1
Q: When you solve a static equilibrium problem, where is the smartest place to put the pivot for the torque equation?
A) Where an unknown force acts, so that force has no torque
B) Always at the center of the object
C) As far from every force as possible
D) It must be at a real hinge or axle
Answer: A
Why: In equilibrium the net torque is zero about every point, so any pivot works. A force acting at the pivot has zero lever arm, so one unknown disappears from the equation.

## QUIZ 12.1
Q: The front wheels of a car carry 60% of its weight and the rear wheels 40%. Where is the car's center of gravity?
A) Closer to the front axle
B) Closer to the rear axle
C) Exactly halfway between the axles
D) Directly above the front axle
Answer: A
Why: Take torques about the center of gravity: the bigger force (front) must have the shorter lever arm, so the center of gravity sits nearer the front.

## QUIZ 12.1
Q: A picture hangs from a single nail and stays still. Where is its center of gravity?
A) Directly below the nail
B) Directly above the nail
C) Level with the nail, off to one side
D) It could be anywhere
Answer: A
Why: The nail's force acts at the nail. If the center of gravity were off to the side, gravity would have a torque about the nail and the picture would swing. Directly below is the stable position.

## DRILL 12.1
Q: A car's front wheels carry \({{F}}\ \text{N}\) and its rear wheels \({{R}}\ \text{N}\). The axles are \({{d}}\ \text{m}\) apart. How far behind the front axle is the center of gravity?

## DRILL 12.1
Q: Two forces of \({{F}}\ \text{N}\) each push on opposite ends of a \({{d}}\ \text{m}\) bar, at right angles to it and in opposite directions. What is the net torque about the bar's center?
Let: F = one of 10, 20, 30; d = one of 0.5, 1, 2
Answer: F*d
Wrong: 0 | The forces cancel, but their torques don't: both turn the bar the same way.
Wrong: F*d/2 | That is one force. Each has lever arm \(\frac d2\), and both torques add.
Wrong: 2*F*d | Each force acts \(\frac d2\) from the center, not \(d\).

# ============================ 12.2 EXAMPLES OF STATIC EQUILIBRIUM ============================

## QUIZ 12.2
Q: A 300 N child sits 2 m from the pivot of a seesaw. Where must a 600 N adult sit to balance it?
A) 1 m from the pivot, on the other side
B) 4 m from the pivot, on the other side
C) 2 m from the pivot, on the other side
D) 1 m from the pivot, on the child's side
Answer: A
Why: The torques must match: \((300)(2) = (600)r\), so \(r = 1\ \text{m}\). The adult sits on the other side so the torques turn opposite ways.

## QUIZ 12.2
Q: Why does your biceps pull many times harder than the weight of a ball held in your hand?
A) It attaches close to the elbow, so its lever arm is short
B) Muscles can only pull at a fraction of their strength
C) The forearm's weight pushes the ball down
D) The biceps pulls at a steep angle away from the arm
Answer: A
Why: About the elbow, the muscle's torque must match the ball's. The ball is far from the elbow and the muscle attaches only a few centimeters away, so the muscle force must be bigger by that ratio.

## QUIZ 12.2
Q: A ladder leans against a smooth (frictionless) wall. What keeps its foot from sliding out?
A) Static friction from the floor
B) The wall's normal force
C) The ladder's weight
D) The floor's normal force
Answer: A
Why: The wall pushes the ladder horizontally away from it. The only horizontal force that can balance that push is static friction at the floor.

## QUIZ 12.2
Q: A painter climbs higher on a ladder leaning against a smooth wall. What happens to the friction the floor must supply?
A) It increases
B) It decreases
C) It stays the same
D) It drops to zero
Answer: A
Why: About the ladder's foot, the painter's weight has a longer lever arm the higher she goes. The wall must push harder to balance it, and friction must match the wall's push. That is why ladders slip when you are near the top.

## QUIZ 12.2
Q: You solve for a hinge force and get a negative number. What does that mean?
A) The force points opposite to the direction you guessed
B) You made a mistake
C) The force is really zero
D) The object can't be in equilibrium
Answer: A
Why: You guess a direction when you draw the free body diagram. A negative answer just says the guess was backward; the size is still right.

## DRILL 12.2

## DRILL 12.2
Q: You hold a \({{w}}\ \text{N}\) ball in your hand \({{L}}\ \text{m}\) from your elbow, forearm level. Your biceps pulls straight up \({{d}}\ \text{m}\) from the elbow. Ignore the forearm's weight. How hard does the biceps pull?

## DRILL 12.2
Q: A uniform \({{W}}\ \text{N}\) ladder leans against a smooth wall. Its top touches the wall \({{h}}\ \text{m}\) up, and its foot is \({{b}}\ \text{m}\) from the wall. How big is the friction force at the floor?

# ============================ 12.3 STRESS, STRAIN, AND ELASTIC MODULUS ============================

## QUIZ 12.3
Q: Which of these has no units?
A) Strain
B) Stress
C) Young's modulus
D) Bulk modulus
Answer: A
Why: Strain is a change in size divided by the original size, like \(\frac{\Delta L}{L_0}\), so the units cancel. Stress and every modulus are in pascals.

## QUIZ 12.3
Q: Two steel wires have the same length and hold the same weight. Wire B has twice the diameter of wire A. How does B's stretch compare with A's?
A) One fourth as much
B) Half as much
C) The same
D) Twice as much
Answer: A
Why: \(\Delta L = \frac{FL_0}{AY}\), and the area grows with the diameter squared. Twice the diameter means four times the area, so one fourth the stretch.

## QUIZ 12.3
Q: Two wires of the same material and thickness hold the same weight. Wire B is twice as long as wire A. How does B's stretch compare with A's?
A) Twice as much
B) The same
C) Half as much
D) Four times as much
Answer: A
Why: The strain \(\frac{\Delta L}{L_0}\) is the same for both, since the stress is the same. A wire twice as long stretches twice as far.

## QUIZ 12.3
Q: You push sideways on the top of a block of gelatin while the bottom stays put, and the top slides over a little. Which modulus describes this?
A) Shear modulus
B) Young's modulus
C) Bulk modulus
D) None, gelatin isn't elastic
Answer: A
Why: A force parallel to a face, with the opposite face held, is shear. Young's modulus is for stretching or squeezing along a length, bulk modulus for squeezing from all sides.

## QUIZ 12.3
Q: Water has a bulk modulus of \(2.2\times10^9\) Pa and steel \(1.6\times10^{11}\) Pa. Which is easier to compress?
A) Water
B) Steel
C) Equally easy
D) Neither can be compressed at all
Answer: A
Why: A smaller bulk modulus means a bigger fractional volume change for the same pressure. Water is about 70 times easier to squeeze than steel, though both are very stiff.

# ============================ 12.4 ELASTICITY AND PLASTICITY ============================

## QUIZ 12.4
Q: In which part of a stress strain diagram does a material return to its original shape when the load is removed?
A) The elastic region
B) The plastic region
C) Past the breaking point
D) Nowhere; materials never fully return
Answer: A
Why: Elastic means it springs back. In the plastic region it keeps a permanent deformation.

## QUIZ 12.4
Q: What is the proportionality limit?
A) The largest stress for which stress is proportional to strain
B) The stress at which the material breaks
C) The stress at which permanent deformation starts
D) The largest strain the material can have
Answer: A
Why: Below it, the stress strain graph is a straight line with slope equal to the modulus. Permanent deformation starts later, at the elastic limit.

## QUIZ 12.4
Q: You bend a paper clip and it stays bent. What happened?
A) The stress went past the elastic limit
B) The stress stayed below the proportionality limit
C) The paper clip's modulus dropped to zero
D) The stress reached zero
Answer: A
Why: A permanent bend is plastic behavior, which happens only once the stress goes past the elastic limit.

## QUIZ 12.4
Q: A wire is loaded past its proportionality limit but not past its elastic limit, then unloaded. What happens?
A) It returns to its original length, but stress and strain weren't proportional near the top of the load
B) It stays permanently stretched
C) It breaks
D) It follows Hooke's law the whole way
Answer: A
Why: Between the two limits the material is still elastic, so it springs back. The graph just isn't a straight line there.

## QUIZ 12.4
Q: Where on a stress strain diagram does a wire snap?
A) At the breaking point, at the end of the plastic region
B) At the proportionality limit
C) At the elastic limit
D) At the start of the elastic region
Answer: A
Why: The wire first stretches elastically, then plastically, and finally breaks at the breaking point.

## DRILL 12.4
