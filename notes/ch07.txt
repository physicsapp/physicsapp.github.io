## SUMMARY 7.1
In short: A force does work on an object when the object moves. For a constant force, \(W = Fd\cos\theta\), where \(\theta\) is the angle between the force and the displacement. A force along the motion does positive work, a force against it does negative work, and a force at right angles to the motion (like the normal force on a level floor) does none. Work is measured in joules, \(1\ \text{J} = 1\ \text{N·m}\). Gravity does \(-mg(y_B - y_A)\), whatever the path. Kinetic friction does \(-f_k\) times the length of the path, so a longer path means more negative work. A spring does \(-\tfrac12k(x_B^2 - x_A^2)\). When the force changes along the way, the work is the area under the force versus position graph.
Watch out: Holding a heavy box still does no work on it, even though your arms get tired. No displacement, no work.

## SUMMARY 7.2
In short: Kinetic energy is the energy of motion, \(K = \tfrac12mv^2\). It goes as the speed squared, so doubling the speed makes it four times as large. It's a scalar and never negative. In terms of momentum, \(K = p^2/(2m)\). The kinetic energy of a system is the sum of the kinetic energies of its parts. Speed depends on the frame of reference, so kinetic energy does too.
Watch out: Kinetic energy has no direction. A car going north and an identical car going south at the same speed have the same \(K\).

## SUMMARY 7.3
In short: The work energy theorem: the net work done on an object equals the change in its kinetic energy, \(W_\text{net} = K_B - K_A\). Add up the work done by every force, or find the net force and multiply by the displacement. Positive net work speeds the object up, negative net work slows it down, and zero net work leaves its speed the same. This connects forces and speeds directly, with no need to find the acceleration or the time.
Watch out: Use the net work from all the forces, not the work of just one. A box pushed at constant speed against friction gains no kinetic energy: your positive work and friction's negative work cancel.

## SUMMARY 7.4
In short: Power is how fast work gets done, \(P = dW/dt\); over a time interval, the average power is \(W/\Delta t\). Its unit is the watt, \(1\ \text{W} = 1\ \text{J/s}\). A force on a moving object delivers power \(P = \vec{F}\cdot\vec{v} = Fv\cos\theta\), so a car climbing a hill at steady speed needs more power the faster it goes. Walking or running up the same stairs takes the same work, but running takes more power. The work done over a time is the area under the power versus time graph.
Watch out: Power and energy are different things. A kilowatt hour is an amount of energy, \(3.6 \times 10^6\ \text{J}\), not a power.

## EQUATIONS 7.1
* Work done by a constant force: \(W = \vec{F}\cdot\vec{d} = Fd\cos\theta\) | \(F\) is the force (N), \(d\) the displacement (m) and \(\theta\) the angle between them, giving work in joules (1 J = 1 N·m). Only the part of the force along the motion does work; a perpendicular force does none.
Dot product in components: \(\vec{A}\cdot\vec{B} = A_xB_x + A_yB_y + A_zB_z\) | \(A_x, A_y, A_z\) and \(B_x, B_y, B_z\) are the components. Multiply matching components and add, which is handy for work when the force and displacement are given in components.
* Work done by a force along a path: \(W_{AB} = \int_A^B \vec{F}\cdot d\vec{r}\) | \(\vec{F}\) is the force (N) and \(d\vec{r}\) a tiny piece of the path (m), from point A to point B. Add up \(\vec{F}\cdot d\vec{r}\) along the whole path to get the work in joules.
Work done by a varying force in one dimension: \(W = \int_{x_A}^{x_B} F_x\,dx\) | \(F_x\) is the force along x (N) and \(x_A\), \(x_B\) the start and end positions (m). The work is the area under the force versus position graph.
* Work done by kinetic friction: \(W_\text{fr} = -f_k\,|\ell_{AB}|\) | \(f_k\) is the kinetic friction force (N) and \(|\ell_{AB}|\) the path length slid (m). Friction always opposes the sliding, so its work is negative and grows with the distance traveled.
* Work done by gravity near Earth: \(W_\text{grav} = -mg(y_B - y_A)\) | \(m\) is the mass (kg), \(g = 9.8\) m/s² and \(y_B - y_A\) the change in height (m). Only the change in height matters, not the path; going up gives negative work.
* Work done by a spring: \(W_\text{spring} = -\tfrac{1}{2}k(x_B^2 - x_A^2)\) | \(k\) is the spring constant (N/m) and \(x_A\), \(x_B\) the start and end stretches from the relaxed length (m). The spring does positive work when it moves back toward its relaxed length.

## EQUATIONS 7.2
* Kinetic energy: \(K = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2 = \frac{p^2}{2m}\) | \(m\) is the mass (kg), \(v\) the speed (m/s) and \(p\) the momentum (kg·m/s), giving energy in joules. It is the energy of motion and is never negative.

## EQUATIONS 7.3
* Work energy theorem: \(W_\text{net} = K_B - K_A\) | \(W_\text{net}\) is the work done by all forces (J) and \(K_A\), \(K_B\) the kinetic energies at the start and end (J). Net work changes the kinetic energy: positive speeds the object up, negative slows it down.

## EQUATIONS 7.4
* Power: \(P = \frac{dW}{dt}\) | \(W\) is the work (J) and \(t\) the time (s), giving power in watts (1 W = 1 J/s). Power is how fast work is done.
* Average power: \(P_\text{avg} = \frac{W}{\Delta t}\) | \(W\) is the work done (J) over the time interval \(\Delta t\) (s), giving watts. The same work done faster takes more power.
* Power delivered by a force: \(P = \vec{F}\cdot\vec{v} = Fv\cos\theta\) | \(F\) is the force (N), \(v\) the velocity (m/s) and \(\theta\) the angle between them, giving watts. Only the part of the force along the velocity delivers power.
Work from power: \(W = \int P\,dt\) | \(P\) is the power (W) and \(t\) the time (s). The work done is the area under the power versus time graph, in joules.

## QUIZ 7.1
Q: You carry a box across a level room at constant velocity. How much work does your upward force do on the box?
A) Zero
B) Positive
C) Negative
D) It depends on how fast you walk
Answer: A
Why: Your force points up and the box moves sideways. At \(90^\circ\), \(\cos\theta = 0\), so the work is zero.

## QUIZ 7.1
Q: A ball is thrown straight up. While it rises, what is the sign of the work done on it by gravity?
A) Positive
B) Negative
C) Zero
D) It changes sign halfway up
Answer: B
Why: Gravity points down and the ball moves up, so \(\theta = 180^\circ\) and \(W = -mgh\).

## QUIZ 7.1
Q: You lift a book from the floor to a shelf, once straight up and once along a winding path. How does the work done by gravity compare?
A) More negative for the winding path
B) Less negative for the winding path
C) The same both times
D) Zero both times
Answer: C
Why: Gravity's work depends only on the change in height: \(W = -mg(y_B - y_A)\). The path doesn't matter.

## QUIZ 7.1
Q: You slide a box across a rough floor from A to B, once in a straight line and once along a longer curved path. How does the work done by kinetic friction compare?
A) More negative on the longer path
B) The same both times
C) Less negative on the longer path
D) Zero both times
Answer: A
Why: Friction always opposes the motion, so its work is \(-f_k\) times the path length. A longer path means more negative work.

## QUIZ 7.1
Q: A satellite moves in a circular orbit at constant speed. How much work does gravity do on it during one quarter of the orbit?
A) Positive
B) Negative
C) Zero
D) It depends on the orbit's radius
Answer: C
Why: Gravity points toward the center, at right angles to the velocity at every moment, so it does no work.

## QUIZ 7.1
Q: Which of these is equal to one joule?
A) \(1\ \text{N·m}\)
B) \(1\ \text{N/m}\)
C) \(1\ \text{kg·m/s}\)
D) \(1\ \text{kg·m/s}^2\)
Answer: A
Why: Work is force times distance, so \(1\ \text{J} = 1\ \text{N·m} = 1\ \text{kg·m}^2/\text{s}^2\). Choice C is a unit of momentum and choice D is a newton.

# ============================ 7.2 KINETIC ENERGY ============================

## QUIZ 7.2
Q: A car doubles its speed. By what factor does its kinetic energy change?
A) 2
B) 4
C) 8
D) \(\sqrt{2}\)
Answer: B
Why: \(K = \tfrac12mv^2\), so doubling \(v\) multiplies \(K\) by \(2^2 = 4\).

## QUIZ 7.2
Q: A truck has twice the mass of a car, and both move at the same speed. How do their kinetic energies compare?
A) The truck has twice as much
B) The truck has four times as much
C) They are equal
D) The truck has half as much
Answer: A
Why: \(K\) is proportional to the mass, so twice the mass at the same speed gives twice the kinetic energy.

## QUIZ 7.2
Q: Can an object's kinetic energy be negative?
A) Yes, when it moves in the negative direction
B) Yes, when it slows down
C) No, never
D) Only in some frames of reference
Answer: C
Why: Mass is positive and \(v^2\) can't be negative, so \(K = \tfrac12mv^2\) is never negative.

## QUIZ 7.2
Q: A ball moves north at 5 m/s. An identical ball moves south at 5 m/s. How do their kinetic energies compare?
A) The north one has more
B) They are equal
C) They are opposite in sign
D) The south one has more
Answer: B
Why: Kinetic energy is a scalar that depends on speed only, not direction.

## QUIZ 7.2
Q: A passenger walks toward the front of a moving train. Compared with her kinetic energy relative to the train, her kinetic energy relative to the ground is
A) Greater
B) Less
C) The same
D) Zero
Answer: A
Why: Relative to the ground her speed is her walking speed plus the train's speed, so her kinetic energy is larger in that frame.

# ============================ 7.3 WORK ENERGY THEOREM ============================

## QUIZ 7.3
Q: The net work done on an object is zero. What can you say about its speed?
A) It stays the same
B) It is zero
C) It increases
D) It decreases
Answer: A
Why: \(W_\text{net} = \Delta K\). Zero net work means no change in kinetic energy, so the speed stays the same, whatever it is.

## QUIZ 7.3
Q: You push a box across a rough floor at constant speed. How does the work you do compare with the work friction does?
A) Equal in size, opposite in sign
B) Yours is larger
C) Friction's is larger
D) Both are zero
Answer: A
Why: The speed doesn't change, so the net work is zero. Your positive work exactly cancels friction's negative work.

## QUIZ 7.3
Q: A car brakes to a stop. What is the sign of the net work done on it?
A) Positive
B) Negative
C) Zero
D) It depends on the direction it was going
Answer: B
Why: The car loses all its kinetic energy, so \(W_\text{net} = \Delta K < 0\).

## QUIZ 7.3
Q: Two identical cars brake with the same force. One was going twice as fast. How do their stopping distances compare?
A) Twice as far
B) Four times as far
C) The same
D) \(\sqrt{2}\) times as far
Answer: B
Why: \(Fd = \tfrac12mv^2\), so the stopping distance goes as \(v^2\). Twice the speed takes four times the distance.

## QUIZ 7.3
Q: A ball is dropped from rest. Ignoring air resistance, how does its speed after falling \(4h\) compare with its speed after falling \(h\)?
A) Twice as fast
B) Four times as fast
C) \(\sqrt{2}\) times as fast
D) Sixteen times as fast
Answer: A
Why: Gravity does work \(mgh\), so \(v = \sqrt{2gh}\). Four times the height gives \(\sqrt{4} = 2\) times the speed.

## DRILL 7.3
Q: A \({{m}}\ \text{kg}\) car moving at \({{v}}\ \text{m/s}\) brakes with a steady force of \({{F}}\ \text{N}\). How far does it go before it stops?
Let: m = one of 1000, 1500, 2000; v = one of 10, 20, 30; F = one of 5000, 10000
Answer: m*v^2/(2*F)
Wrong: m*v^2/F | The kinetic energy is \(\tfrac12mv^2\). Include the \(\tfrac12\).
Wrong: m*v/(2*F) | The speed is squared.
Wrong: v^2/(2*F) | Kinetic energy needs the mass.
Unit: m
Work: Fd = \tfrac12mv^2\ \Rightarrow\ d = \frac{mv^2}{2F} = \frac{({{m}})({{v}})^2}{2({{F}})} = {{answer}}\ \text{m}

## DRILL 7.3

## DRILL 7.3
Q: A \({{F}}\ \text{N}\) force pushes a \({{m}}\ \text{kg}\) box from rest for \({{d}}\ \text{m}\) along a frictionless floor. How fast is the box moving at the end?

## DRILL 7.3
Q: You push a \({{m}}\ \text{kg}\) box from rest with a horizontal \({{F}}\ \text{N}\) force for \({{d}}\ \text{m}\). The floor has \(\mu_k = {{mu}}\). How much kinetic energy does the box have at the end? Use \(g = 10\ \text{m/s}^2\).

## DRILL 7.3
Q: A \({{m}}\ \text{kg}\) ball moving at \({{v1}}\ \text{m/s}\) has \({{W}}\ \text{J}\) of net work done on it. How fast is it moving afterward?

## DRILL 7.3
Q: A \({{m}}\ \text{kg}\) block is pressed \({{x}}\ \text{m}\) into a spring with \(k = {{k}}\ \text{N/m}\) on a frictionless floor, then released. How fast does it leave the spring?

## GRAPH 7.3
Q: The graph shows the velocity of a 1 kg cart. How much net work is done on it from \(t = 0\) to \(t = 4\ \text{s}\)?

# ============================ 7.4 POWER ============================

## QUIZ 7.4
Q: You walk up a flight of stairs, then run up the same stairs. Compare the work you do against gravity and your power.
A) Same work, more power running
B) More work and more power running
C) Same work, same power
D) More work running, same power
Answer: A
Why: The work is \(mgh\) either way. Running does it in less time, so the power, \(W/\Delta t\), is larger.

## QUIZ 7.4
Q: Your electric bill charges by the kilowatt hour. What does a kilowatt hour measure?
A) Energy
B) Power
C) Force
D) Time
Answer: A
Why: A kilowatt hour is a power times a time: \((1000\ \text{J/s})(3600\ \text{s}) = 3.6 \times 10^6\ \text{J}\) of energy.

## QUIZ 7.4
Q: A car climbs a hill at steady speed. If it climbs at twice the speed, against the same forces, how does the engine's power change?
A) It doubles
B) It quadruples
C) It stays the same
D) It halves
Answer: A
Why: \(P = Fv\). The same force at twice the speed takes twice the power.

## QUIZ 7.4
Q: A string whirls a ball in a horizontal circle at constant speed. What power does the tension deliver to the ball?
A) Zero
B) \(Tv\)
C) \(Tv^2\)
D) It depends on the radius
Answer: A
Why: The tension points toward the center, at right angles to the velocity, so \(P = \vec{F}\cdot\vec{v} = 0\).

## QUIZ 7.4
Q: Which of these equals one watt?
A) \(1\ \text{J/s}\)
B) \(1\ \text{J·s}\)
C) \(1\ \text{N·s}\)
D) \(1\ \text{N/m}\)
Answer: A
Why: Power is work per time, so \(1\ \text{W} = 1\ \text{J/s}\).

## DRILL 7.4
Q: A \({{m}}\ \text{kg}\) climber goes up \({{h}}\ \text{m}\) at steady speed in \({{t}}\ \text{s}\). What average power does she supply against gravity? Use \(g = 10\ \text{m/s}^2\).

Wrong: m*10*h | That's the work. Divide by the time.

Unit: W
Work: P = \frac{mgh}{\Delta t} = \frac{({{m}})(10)({{h}})}{{{t}}} = {{answer}}\ \text{W}

## DRILL 7.4

Wrong: F*v^2 | The velocity isn't squared.
