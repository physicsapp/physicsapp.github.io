## SUMMARY 15.1
In short: An oscillation repeats itself. The period \(T\) is the time for one cycle and the frequency \(f = \frac1T\) is cycles per second (Hz). Simple harmonic motion (SHM) happens when the restoring force is proportional to the displacement, like a mass on a spring, \(F = -kx\). Then \(x(t) = A\cos(\omega t + \phi)\) with angular frequency \(\omega = 2\pi f = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}\), so \(T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{m}{k}}\). The speed is greatest at equilibrium (\(v_\text{max} = A\omega\)) and the acceleration is greatest at the ends (\(a_\text{max} = A\omega^2\)).
Watch out: The period of SHM doesn't depend on the amplitude. A bigger swing just moves faster.

## SUMMARY 15.2
In short: With no friction, the energy of a mass on a spring stays the same while it trades back and forth between the spring's potential energy and the block's kinetic energy: \(E = \frac12kx^2 + \frac12mv^2 = \frac12kA^2\). At the ends (\(x = \pm A\)) it is all potential; at equilibrium it is all kinetic, \(\frac12mv_\text{max}^2 = \frac12kA^2\). Solving for the speed at any position gives \(v = \pm\sqrt{\frac{k}{m}(A^2 - x^2)}\).
Watch out: The energy goes as amplitude squared. Double the amplitude and the energy goes up 4 times.

## SUMMARY 15.3
In short: SHM is the shadow of circular motion. A point moving in a circle of radius \(A\) at constant angular velocity \(\omega\) has an \(x\) component \(x = A\cos(\omega t + \phi)\), exactly SHM. The point's speed \(A\omega\) is the shadow's maximum speed, and its centripetal acceleration \(A\omega^2\) is the shadow's maximum acceleration. This is where the "angular" in angular frequency comes from.
Watch out: The shadow moves fastest as it passes the center, where the point on the circle is moving straight across, and stops at the ends.

## SUMMARY 15.4
In short: A simple pendulum is a small mass on a light string of length \(L\). For small swings (about 15° or less, so \(\sin\theta \approx \theta\)), it moves in SHM with \(\omega = \sqrt{\frac{g}{L}}\) and period \(T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{L}{g}}\). The period doesn't depend on the mass or the amplitude, which is why pendulums keep good time and can measure \(g\). Any rigid object swinging about a pivot is a physical pendulum, \(T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{I}{mgL}}\), with \(L\) the distance from the pivot to the center of mass. A torsional pendulum twists back and forth: \(T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{I}{\kappa}}\).
Watch out: To double a pendulum's period, make it 4 times longer, since \(T\) goes as \(\sqrt{L}\).

## SUMMARY 15.5
In short: Real oscillators lose energy to friction or drag, which is damping. With a damping force \(-bv\), light damping (underdamped) still oscillates, at a slightly lower \(\omega = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 - \left(\frac{b}{2m}\right)^2}\), with an amplitude that shrinks as \(e^{-bt/2m}\). Critical damping returns to equilibrium fastest without oscillating, which is what car shock absorbers aim for. Heavier damping (overdamped) returns slowly without oscillating.
Watch out: The amplitude shrinks, but in light damping the period barely changes.

## SUMMARY 15.6
In short: A driving force at frequency \(\omega\) makes an oscillator settle into motion at that frequency. The amplitude is largest when the driving frequency is close to the natural frequency \(\omega_0 = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}\): this is resonance. Less damping gives a taller, narrower resonance peak. Pushing a child on a swing works because you push once per swing, at its natural frequency.
Watch out: The system settles at the driving frequency, not its own natural frequency. Resonance is when the two match.

## EQUATIONS 15.1
* Frequency and period: \(f = \dfrac1T\) | Frequency \(f\) (Hz, cycles per second) and period \(T\) (s, time for one cycle) are reciprocals.
* Angular frequency: \(\omega = 2\pi f = \dfrac{2\pi}{T}\) | Angular frequency \(\omega\) (rad/s) packs one cycle into \(2\pi\) radians.
* Position in SHM: \(x(t) = A\cos(\omega t + \phi)\) | Displacement (m) with amplitude \(A\) (m), angular frequency \(\omega\) (rad/s), and phase shift \(\phi\) (rad) set by where it starts.
Velocity in SHM: \(v(t) = -A\omega\sin(\omega t + \phi)\) | The derivative of \(x(t)\). It is largest, \(A\omega\), when \(x = 0\).
Acceleration in SHM: \(a(t) = -A\omega^2\cos(\omega t + \phi)\) | The derivative of \(v(t)\), equal to \(-\omega^2x\). It always points back toward equilibrium.
* Maximum speed and acceleration: \(v_\text{max} = A\omega,\quad a_\text{max} = A\omega^2\) | The speed (m/s) peaks at equilibrium; the acceleration (m/s²) peaks at the ends, \(x = \pm A\).
* Mass on a spring: \(\omega = \sqrt{\dfrac{k}{m}}\) | Angular frequency (rad/s) of a mass \(m\) (kg) on a spring with constant \(k\) (N/m).
* Period of a mass on a spring: \(T = 2\pi\sqrt{\dfrac{m}{k}}\) | Time (s) for one cycle. More mass is slower; a stiffer spring is faster. It doesn't depend on the amplitude.
Frequency of a mass on a spring: \(f = \dfrac1{2\pi}\sqrt{\dfrac{k}{m}}\) | Cycles per second (Hz), the reciprocal of the period.

## EQUATIONS 15.2
* Energy in SHM: \(E = \tfrac12kx^2 + \tfrac12mv^2 = \tfrac12kA^2\) | Total energy (J) of a mass on a spring: spring potential plus kinetic, equal to the potential energy at the ends.
* Speed at a position: \(v = \pm\sqrt{\dfrac{k}{m}(A^2 - x^2)}\) | Speed (m/s) of the mass at displacement \(x\) (m), from energy conservation. At \(x = 0\) it is \(A\omega\).

## EQUATIONS 15.3
Shadow of circular motion: \(x(t) = A\cos(\omega t + \phi)\) | The \(x\) component of a point moving in a circle of radius \(A\) (m) at angular velocity \(\omega\) (rad/s) is SHM.
Shadow's top speed and acceleration: \(v_\text{max} = A\omega,\quad a_\text{max} = A\omega^2\) | The point's speed on the circle and its centripetal acceleration.

## EQUATIONS 15.4
Pendulum equation: \(\dfrac{d^2\theta}{dt^2} = -\dfrac{g}{L}\theta\) | For small angles \(\theta\) (rad), \(\sin\theta \approx \theta\) and the pendulum's equation has the SHM form.
* Simple pendulum: \(\omega = \sqrt{\dfrac{g}{L}}\) | Angular frequency (rad/s) of a pendulum of length \(L\) (m) swinging through small angles.
* Period of a simple pendulum: \(T = 2\pi\sqrt{\dfrac{L}{g}}\) | Time (s) for one swing back and forth. It doesn't depend on the mass or (for small swings) the amplitude.
* Physical pendulum: \(T = 2\pi\sqrt{\dfrac{I}{mgL}}\) | A rigid object of mass \(m\) (kg) and moment of inertia \(I\) (kg·m²) about the pivot, with its center of mass \(L\) (m) from the pivot.
Torsional pendulum: \(T = 2\pi\sqrt{\dfrac{I}{\kappa}}\) | An object twisting on a wire with torsion constant \(\kappa\) (N·m/rad).

## EQUATIONS 15.5
Damped oscillator: \(m\dfrac{d^2x}{dt^2} + b\dfrac{dx}{dt} + kx = 0\) | Newton's second law with a spring force \(-kx\) and a damping force \(-bv\), where \(b\) is in kg/s.
* Underdamped motion: \(x(t) = A_0e^{-bt/2m}\cos(\omega t + \phi)\) | It still oscillates, but the amplitude shrinks by a factor of \(e\) every \(\frac{2m}{b}\) seconds.
* Damped angular frequency: \(\omega = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 - \left(\dfrac{b}{2m}\right)^2}\) | A little slower than the natural angular frequency \(\omega_0 = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}\). When \(\frac{b}{2m} = \omega_0\) it is critically damped.

## EQUATIONS 15.6
Driven oscillator: \(m\dfrac{d^2x}{dt^2} = -kx - b\dfrac{dx}{dt} + F_0\sin(\omega t)\) | A damped mass on a spring pushed by a force of size \(F_0\) (N) at angular frequency \(\omega\) (rad/s).
* Amplitude of a driven oscillator: \(A = \dfrac{F_0}{\sqrt{m^2(\omega^2 - \omega_0^2)^2 + b^2\omega^2}}\) | Biggest when \(\omega\) is near \(\omega_0\) (resonance); smaller damping \(b\) makes the peak taller.

# ============================ 15.1 SIMPLE HARMONIC MOTION ============================

## QUIZ 15.1
Q: A mass on a spring oscillates. You pull it farther before letting go, doubling the amplitude. What happens to the period?
A) It stays the same
B) It doubles
C) It is cut in half
D) It goes up by \(\sqrt2\)
Answer: A
Why: \(T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{m}{k}}\) has no amplitude in it. The mass travels farther but moves faster, and the time per cycle is unchanged.

## QUIZ 15.1
Q: Where is a mass on a spring moving fastest?
A) Passing through equilibrium
B) At the ends of its motion
C) Halfway between equilibrium and an end
D) It moves at constant speed
Answer: A
Why: At equilibrium all the energy is kinetic, so the speed is greatest, \(v_\text{max} = A\omega\). At the ends it stops for an instant.

## QUIZ 15.1
Q: Where is the acceleration of a mass on a spring greatest?
A) At the ends of its motion
B) Passing through equilibrium
C) Halfway between equilibrium and an end
D) It is the same everywhere
Answer: A
Why: The spring force \(-kx\) is largest at the biggest stretch or squeeze, so \(a = -\omega^2x\) peaks at \(x = \pm A\). At equilibrium the force and acceleration are zero.

## QUIZ 15.1
Q: The mass on a spring is replaced by one 4 times heavier. What happens to the period?
A) It doubles
B) It goes up 4 times
C) It is cut in half
D) It stays the same
Answer: A
Why: \(T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{m}{k}}\), so 4 times the mass gives \(\sqrt4 = 2\) times the period.

## QUIZ 15.1
Q: What is needed for an oscillation to be simple harmonic motion?
A) A restoring force proportional to the displacement
B) A constant restoring force
C) No forces at all
D) A force that always points the same way
Answer: A
Why: SHM needs \(F = -kx\): the force points back toward equilibrium and grows in proportion to how far the object is displaced.

## QUIZ 15.2
Q: A mass on a spring with no friction is at the far end of its motion. What form is its energy in?
A) All spring potential energy
B) All kinetic energy
C) Half potential, half kinetic
D) It has no energy there
Answer: A
Why: At \(x = \pm A\) the mass stops for an instant, so \(K = 0\) and all the energy is \(\frac12kA^2\).

## QUIZ 15.2
Q: The amplitude of a mass on a spring is doubled. What happens to its total energy?
A) It goes up 4 times
B) It doubles
C) It stays the same
D) It goes up \(\sqrt2\) times
Answer: A
Why: \(E = \frac12kA^2\), so doubling \(A\) multiplies the energy by \(2^2 = 4\).

## QUIZ 15.2
Q: As a mass on a spring passes through equilibrium, its energy is
A) all kinetic
B) all potential
C) half kinetic and half potential
D) zero
Answer: A
Why: At \(x = 0\) the spring is relaxed, so \(U = 0\) and all of \(E\) is kinetic. That's where the speed is greatest.

## QUIZ 15.2
Q: For a mass on a spring with amplitude \(A\), at which displacement is the kinetic energy equal to the potential energy?
A) About \(0.71A\)
B) \(0.5A\)
C) \(0.25A\)
D) \(A\)
Answer: A
Why: Each is half the total: \(\frac12kx^2 = \frac12\left(\frac12kA^2\right)\), so \(x = \frac{A}{\sqrt2} \approx 0.71A\). At \(0.5A\) the potential energy is only a quarter of the total.

# ============================ 15.3 COMPARING SIMPLE HARMONIC MOTION AND CIRCULAR MOTION ============================

## QUIZ 15.3
Q: A ball sits on the edge of a turntable spinning at a steady rate. A light from the side casts its shadow on a wall. How does the shadow move?
A) In simple harmonic motion
B) At constant speed back and forth
C) In a circle
D) It doesn't move
Answer: A
Why: The shadow shows only one component of the circular motion, \(x = A\cos(\omega t + \phi)\), which is exactly SHM.

## QUIZ 15.3
Q: In that same setup, what is the shadow's top speed?
A) The ball's speed on the circle, \(A\omega\)
B) Half the ball's speed
C) Twice the ball's speed
D) Zero
Answer: A
Why: As the shadow passes the center, the ball is moving straight across, parallel to the wall, so the shadow moves at the ball's full speed \(A\omega\).

## QUIZ 15.3
Q: Where is the shadow's acceleration largest?
A) At the ends of its motion
B) At the center
C) It is the same everywhere
D) It has no acceleration
Answer: A
Why: At the ends, the ball's centripetal acceleration \(A\omega^2\) points straight along the shadow's line, so the shadow's acceleration is the full \(A\omega^2\).

## QUIZ 15.3
Q: Why is \(\omega\) in SHM called the angular frequency?
A) It is the angular velocity of the circular motion whose shadow is the SHM
B) Because the object moves in an arc
C) It measures the angle of the spring
D) It is just a name; it has no meaning
Answer: A
Why: SHM is the projection of motion around a circle at angular velocity \(\omega\) rad/s. One cycle is one trip around, \(2\pi\) rad.

# ============================ 15.4 PENDULUMS ============================

## QUIZ 15.4
Q: Two pendulums have the same length, but one bob has twice the mass. How do their periods compare?
A) They are the same
B) The heavier one has the longer period
C) The heavier one has the shorter period
D) The heavier one's period is \(\sqrt2\) times longer
Answer: A
Why: \(T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{L}{g}}\) has no mass in it. A heavier bob needs more force, and gravity gives it exactly that much more.

## QUIZ 15.4
Q: A pendulum is made 4 times longer. What happens to its period?
A) It doubles
B) It goes up 4 times
C) It is cut in half
D) It stays the same
Answer: A
Why: \(T \propto \sqrt{L}\), so 4 times the length gives \(\sqrt4 = 2\) times the period.

## QUIZ 15.4
Q: A pendulum clock is taken to the Moon, where \(g\) is smaller. What happens?
A) Its period gets longer, so it runs slow
B) Its period gets shorter, so it runs fast
C) It keeps the same time
D) It stops swinging
Answer: A
Why: \(T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{L}{g}}\), so a smaller \(g\) gives a longer period. Each tick takes longer.

## QUIZ 15.4
Q: Why does the pendulum period formula only work for small swings?
A) It uses \(\sin\theta \approx \theta\), which is only accurate for small angles
B) Large swings break the string
C) Air resistance matters only for large swings
D) The mass changes for large swings
Answer: A
Why: The restoring torque goes as \(\sin\theta\). Only when \(\sin\theta \approx \theta\) (about 15° or less) is the motion SHM.

## QUIZ 15.4
Q: For a physical pendulum, what is \(L\) in \(T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{I}{mgL}}\)?
A) The distance from the pivot to the center of mass
B) The total length of the object
C) The radius of the object
D) The length of the string
Answer: A
Why: Gravity acts at the center of mass, so the torque arm is the distance from the pivot to the center of mass.

# ============================ 15.5 DAMPED OSCILLATIONS ============================

## QUIZ 15.5
Q: A mass on a spring swings in air with a little friction. What happens over time?
A) The amplitude gets smaller as energy is lost
B) The period gets much longer
C) The amplitude stays the same
D) The amplitude grows
Answer: A
Why: Damping takes energy away, so the amplitude shrinks as \(e^{-bt/2m}\). For light damping the period barely changes.

## QUIZ 15.5
Q: What does a car's shock absorber aim for?
A) Critical damping: back to equilibrium fastest, with no bouncing
B) No damping, so the ride is smooth
C) Overdamping, so it never moves
D) Underdamping, so it bounces a few times
Answer: A
Why: Critical damping returns the car to level as quickly as possible without oscillating.

## QUIZ 15.5
Q: How does an overdamped system return to equilibrium?
A) Slowly, without oscillating
B) Quickly, with many oscillations
C) It never returns
D) Faster than a critically damped system
Answer: A
Why: With very heavy damping, like a spring in thick honey, the mass creeps back without overshooting, and more slowly than with critical damping.

# ============================ 15.6 FORCED OSCILLATIONS ============================

## QUIZ 15.6
Q: When does a driven oscillator have its largest amplitude?
A) When the driving frequency is close to its natural frequency
B) When the driving frequency is very high
C) When the driving frequency is very low
D) The amplitude doesn't depend on the driving frequency
Answer: A
Why: That is resonance: each push arrives in step with the motion and adds energy.

## QUIZ 15.6
Q: The damping of a driven oscillator is increased. What happens to the resonance peak?
A) It gets lower and wider
B) It gets taller and narrower
C) It moves to a much higher frequency
D) Nothing changes
Answer: A
Why: More damping removes more energy each cycle, so the largest amplitude is smaller and the response is spread over a wider range of frequencies.

## QUIZ 15.6
Q: How should you push a child on a swing to make it go high?
A) Once each swing, in time with its natural motion
B) As fast as possible
C) At random times
D) Once every 10 swings
Answer: A
Why: Pushing at the swing's natural frequency is resonance: every push adds energy instead of fighting the motion.
