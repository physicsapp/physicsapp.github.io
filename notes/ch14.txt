## SUMMARY 14.1
In short: A fluid (a liquid or a gas) flows and takes the shape of its container. Density is mass per volume, \(\rho = \frac{m}{V}\) (kg/m³); water is about \(1000\ \text{kg/m}^3\). Pressure is force per area, \(p = \frac{F}{A}\), in pascals (1 Pa = 1 N/m²); a fluid pushes at right angles on every surface it touches, equally in all directions at a point. Going down into a fluid of constant density, pressure grows steadily: \(p = p_0 + \rho gh\). The shape of the container doesn't matter, only the depth.
Watch out: The \(h\) in \(\rho gh\) is depth below the surface, measured straight down, not the distance along a slanted wall or the height above the bottom.

## SUMMARY 14.2
In short: Gauge pressure is pressure above atmospheric; absolute pressure includes the atmosphere: \(p_\text{abs} = p_\text{g} + p_\text{atm}\), with \(p_\text{atm} \approx 1.01 \times 10^5\ \text{Pa}\). A tire gauge reads gauge pressure, so a flat tire reads zero even though the air inside is at atmospheric pressure. An open tube manometer shows gauge pressure as a height difference, \(p_\text{g} = \rho gh\). A mercury barometer measures the atmosphere itself: the air holds up a column about 760 mm tall.
Watch out: Gauge pressure can be negative (a partial vacuum), but absolute pressure never is.

## SUMMARY 14.3
In short: Pascal's principle: a change in pressure applied to an enclosed fluid reaches every part of the fluid and the container walls undiminished. In a hydraulic press both pistons feel the same pressure, so \(\frac{F_1}{A_1} = \frac{F_2}{A_2}\): a small force on a small piston makes a big force on a big piston. Energy is still conserved: the small piston moves farther, \(A_1d_1 = A_2d_2\), so the work in equals the work out.
Watch out: A hydraulic lift multiplies force, not energy. Push 10 times less and you push 10 times farther.

## SUMMARY 14.4
In short: Archimedes' principle: a fluid pushes up on a submerged object with a buoyant force equal to the weight of the fluid it displaces, \(F_\text{B} = \rho_\text{fluid}V_\text{sub}g\). It comes from the pressure being larger on the bottom of the object than on the top. An object floats when it is less dense than the fluid; then it sinks just far enough that \(F_\text{B}\) equals its weight, and the fraction under the surface is \(\frac{V_\text{sub}}{V} = \frac{\rho_\text{obj}}{\rho_\text{fluid}}\). An object denser than the fluid sinks, but still feels \(F_\text{B}\), so it seems lighter.
Watch out: Use the volume of fluid displaced (the part under the surface) and the fluid's density, not the object's.

## SUMMARY 14.5
In short: In steady flow, the flow rate is the volume passing a point per second, \(Q = \frac{dV}{dt} = Av\) (m³/s). For an incompressible fluid, what goes in must come out, so \(A_1v_1 = A_2v_2\) (the continuity equation): where a pipe narrows, the fluid speeds up. Squeeze a garden hose and the water jets out faster. For a gas whose density changes, \(\rho_1A_1v_1 = \rho_2A_2v_2\) (mass flow rate stays the same).
Watch out: Area goes as radius squared. Halve a pipe's radius and the speed goes up 4 times, not 2.

## SUMMARY 14.6
In short: Bernoulli's equation is energy conservation per volume for a fluid with no viscosity: \(p + \frac12\rho v^2 + \rho gy\) is the same everywhere along a streamline. At the same height, faster fluid has lower pressure (Bernoulli's principle): air speeding over a curved wing, the shower curtain pulled inward, a narrowing pipe with lower pressure in the narrow part. Torricelli's theorem: water leaving a hole a depth \(h\) below an open surface comes out at \(v = \sqrt{2gh}\), as fast as if it had fallen \(h\).
Watch out: Faster flow means lower pressure, not higher. In the narrow part of a pipe the water moves faster and its pressure is lower, even though it may seem it should be squeezed harder.

## SUMMARY 14.7
In short: Real fluids have viscosity \(\eta\), a kind of internal friction (Pa·s). In laminar flow the layers slide smoothly past each other; at high speeds the flow turns turbulent, with eddies. Through a pipe, Poiseuille's law gives \(Q = \frac{(p_2 - p_1)}{R}\) with resistance \(R = \frac{8\eta l}{\pi r^4}\), so the flow depends very strongly on the radius: double the radius and the flow goes up 16 times. This is why a slightly narrowed artery makes the heart work much harder.
Watch out: The radius is to the fourth power in Poiseuille's law, unlike the square in the continuity equation.

## EQUATIONS 14.1
* Density: \(\rho = \dfrac{m}{V}\) | Mass \(m\) (kg) per volume \(V\) (m³), in kg/m³. Water is about \(1000\ \text{kg/m}^3\).
* Pressure: \(p = \dfrac{F}{A}\) | Force \(F\) (N) at right angles to an area \(A\) (m²), in pascals (1 Pa = 1 N/m²).
* Pressure at a depth: \(p = p_0 + \rho gh\) | Pressure (Pa) a depth \(h\) (m) below a surface at pressure \(p_0\), in a fluid of density \(\rho\). Only the depth matters, not the container's shape.
Change of pressure with height: \(\dfrac{dp}{dy} = -\rho g\) | Going up (\(y\) increasing) in a fluid of density \(\rho\) lowers the pressure by \(\rho g\) per meter.

## EQUATIONS 14.2
* Absolute pressure: \(p_\text{abs} = p_\text{g} + p_\text{atm}\) | Gauge pressure \(p_\text{g}\) (Pa) is what a gauge reads, above atmospheric pressure \(p_\text{atm} \approx 1.01 \times 10^5\ \text{Pa}\).
Manometer or barometer: \(p = \rho gh\) | A column of liquid of density \(\rho\) and height difference \(h\) (m) balances a pressure difference \(p\) (Pa).

## EQUATIONS 14.3
* Pascal's principle: \(\dfrac{F_1}{A_1} = \dfrac{F_2}{A_2}\) | Both pistons of a hydraulic press, of areas \(A_1\) and \(A_2\) (m²), feel the same pressure, so the forces (N) scale with the areas.
Pistons move different distances: \(A_1d_1 = A_2d_2\) | The fluid volume pushed out of one cylinder fills the other, so the small piston moves farther (m) and the work in equals the work out.

## EQUATIONS 14.4
* Buoyant force: \(F_\text{B} = \rho_\text{fluid}V_\text{sub}\,g\) | The weight (N) of the fluid displaced: fluid density \(\rho_\text{fluid}\) (kg/m³) times the submerged volume \(V_\text{sub}\) (m³) times \(g\).
* Fraction submerged: \(\dfrac{V_\text{sub}}{V} = \dfrac{\rho_\text{obj}}{\rho_\text{fluid}}\) | For a floating object of density \(\rho_\text{obj}\), the fraction of its volume below the surface.

## EQUATIONS 14.5
Volume flow rate: \(Q = \dfrac{dV}{dt}\) | The volume (m³) passing a point per second, in m³/s.
* Flow rate and speed: \(Q = Av\) | Fluid moving at average speed \(v\) (m/s) through a cross section of area \(A\) (m²).
* Continuity equation: \(A_1v_1 = A_2v_2\) | For an incompressible fluid, the flow rate is the same everywhere in a pipe, so a narrower part has faster flow.
Continuity, general form: \(\rho_1A_1v_1 = \rho_2A_2v_2\) | The mass flow rate (kg/s) stays the same even when the density changes, as in a gas.

## EQUATIONS 14.6
* Bernoulli's equation: \(p + \tfrac12\rho v^2 + \rho gy = \text{constant}\) | Along a streamline of a fluid with no viscosity: pressure \(p\) (Pa), kinetic energy per volume, and potential energy per volume add to the same total.
* Bernoulli's principle: \(p_1 + \tfrac12\rho v_1^2 = p_2 + \tfrac12\rho v_2^2\) | At the same height, where the fluid moves faster its pressure is lower.
Torricelli's theorem: \(v = \sqrt{2gh}\) | Speed (m/s) of fluid leaving a hole a depth \(h\) (m) below an open surface, the same as falling freely through \(h\).

## EQUATIONS 14.7
Viscosity: \(\eta = \dfrac{FL}{vA}\) | Force \(F\) (N) to slide a plate of area \(A\) (m²) at speed \(v\) (m/s) over a fluid layer of thickness \(L\) (m). Units Pa·s.
Resistance to flow: \(R = \dfrac{8\eta l}{\pi r^4}\) | Resistance of a pipe of length \(l\) (m) and radius \(r\) (m) to fluid of viscosity \(\eta\) (Pa·s).
* Poiseuille's law: \(Q = \dfrac{(p_2 - p_1)\pi r^4}{8\eta l}\) | Laminar flow rate (m³/s) through a pipe driven by a pressure difference (Pa). The \(r^4\) makes radius matter enormously.

## QUIZ 14.1
Q: Three open containers of different shapes, a wide tub, a narrow tube, and a cone, are filled with water to the same depth. Where is the pressure at the bottom greatest?
A) It is the same in all three
B) The wide tub, since it holds the most water
C) The narrow tube
D) The cone
Answer: A
Why: \(p = p_0 + \rho gh\) depends only on the depth \(h\), not on the shape of the container or how much water it holds.

## QUIZ 14.1
Q: Which unit is the same as one pascal?
A) \(\text{N/m}^2\)
B) \(\text{N·m}\)
C) \(\text{kg/m}^3\)
D) \(\text{N/m}\)
Answer: A
Why: Pressure is force per area, \(p = \frac{F}{A}\), so 1 Pa = 1 N/m². Choice C is the unit of density.

## QUIZ 14.1
Q: Why does a sharp knife cut better than a dull one, pushed with the same force?
A) The edge touches a smaller area, so the pressure is larger
B) The sharp knife pushes with more force
C) The dull knife has more friction
D) The pressure is the same; the sharp knife is just harder
Answer: A
Why: \(p = \frac{F}{A}\). The same force on a much smaller area gives a much larger pressure.

## QUIZ 14.1
Q: In a lake, the extra pressure from the water above (beyond the air's pressure) is \(p\) at a depth of 10 m. What is it at 20 m?
A) \(2p\)
B) \(4p\)
C) \(p\)
D) \(\frac12p\)
Answer: A
Why: The water's part is \(\rho gh\), which grows in proportion to the depth. Twice as deep gives twice as much.

## QUIZ 14.1
Q: A uniform block of wood is cut in half. What is the density of each half?
A) The same as the whole block
B) Half the density of the whole block
C) Twice the density of the whole block
D) It depends on how it was cut
Answer: A
Why: \(\rho = \frac{m}{V}\). Each half has half the mass and half the volume, so the ratio is unchanged.

D) 101 kPa

## QUIZ 14.2
Q: Why do barometers use mercury instead of water?

B) Mercury doesn't evaporate at all
C) Water can't hold up a column against the atmosphere
D) Mercury is lighter, so it rises higher
Answer: A
Why: The atmosphere holds up a column with \(\rho gh = p_\text{atm}\). A denser liquid needs a shorter column: about 0.76 m of mercury against about 10 m of water.

## QUIZ 14.2
Q: Which of these can be negative?
A) Gauge pressure, but not absolute pressure
B) Absolute pressure, but not gauge pressure
C) Both
D) Neither
Answer: A
Why: Gauge pressure is measured from atmospheric pressure, so a partial vacuum (like drinking through a straw) has a negative gauge pressure. Absolute pressure can't go below zero.

# ============================ 14.3 PASCAL'S PRINCIPLE AND HYDRAULICS ============================

## QUIZ 14.3
Q: What does a hydraulic lift multiply?
A) Force, but not energy
B) Energy
C) Both force and energy
D) Pressure
Answer: A
Why: The big piston gets a bigger force, but it moves a shorter distance, so the work out equals the work in. The pressure is the same on both pistons.

## QUIZ 14.3
Q: You squeeze the middle of a sealed, full plastic water bottle. Where does the pressure inside go up?
A) Everywhere in the water, by the same amount
B) Only where you squeeze
C) Only at the bottom
D) Nowhere; water can't be compressed
Answer: A
Why: Pascal's principle: a change in pressure on an enclosed fluid reaches every part of it undiminished.

## QUIZ 14.3
Q: In a hydraulic press, the large piston has 10 times the area of the small one. The small piston is pushed down 10 cm. How far does the large piston rise?
A) 1 cm
B) 10 cm
C) 100 cm
D) 0.1 cm
Answer: A
Why: The volume of fluid pushed out, \(A_1d_1\), fills \(A_2d_2\). Ten times the area means one tenth the distance.

## QUIZ 14.3
Q: Why is air in a car's brake lines dangerous?
A) Air compresses, so pushing the pedal squeezes the air instead of pushing the brake pistons
B) Air makes the fluid heavier
C) Air raises the pressure too much
D) Air makes the brakes grab too hard
Answer: A
Why: Hydraulics work because the liquid barely compresses, so the pedal's pressure reaches the wheels. A gas bubble just gets smaller and the pedal feels soft.

## DRILL 14.3
Q: A hydraulic press has a small piston of area \({{A1}}\ \text{m}^2\) and a large piston of area \({{A2}}\ \text{m}^2\). A force of \({{F1}}\ \text{N}\) pushes on the small piston. What force does the large piston exert?

# ============================ 14.4 ARCHIMEDES' PRINCIPLE AND BUOYANCY ============================

## QUIZ 14.4
Q: What does the buoyant force on a submerged object equal?
A) The weight of the fluid the object displaces
B) The weight of the object
C) The volume of the object
D) The pressure at the object's depth
Answer: A
Why: Archimedes' principle: \(F_\text{B} = \rho_\text{fluid}V_\text{sub}g\), the weight of the displaced fluid. It equals the object's weight only when the object floats.

## QUIZ 14.4
Q: Ice has a density of about \(920\ \text{kg/m}^3\). About how much of a floating ice cube is under the water?
A) About 92%
B) About 8%
C) About 50%
D) All of it
Answer: A
Why: For a floating object, \(\frac{V_\text{sub}}{V} = \frac{\rho_\text{obj}}{\rho_\text{fluid}} = \frac{920}{1000} = 0.92\). That's why most of an iceberg is hidden.

## QUIZ 14.4
Q: Steel is much denser than water. How does a steel ship float?
A) Its hollow hull displaces a weight of water equal to the ship's weight
B) Steel becomes less dense in water
C) The air pushes up on the deck
D) It moves fast enough to stay up
Answer: A
Why: The hull encloses a lot of air, so the ship's average density is less than water's. It sinks until the water it displaces weighs as much as the whole ship.

## QUIZ 14.4
Q: An aluminum block and a lead block of the same size are held completely under water. Which has the larger buoyant force?
A) They are the same
B) The lead block
C) The aluminum block
D) It depends on the depth
Answer: A
Why: Both displace the same volume of water, so \(F_\text{B} = \rho_\text{water}Vg\) is the same. The lead block is heavier, so it sinks harder, but the push up is equal.

## QUIZ 14.4
Q: Where does the buoyant force come from?
A) The water pressure on the bottom of the object is greater than on the top
B) The object pushes the water up
C) Water molecules stick to the object
D) Gravity is weaker under water
Answer: A
Why: Pressure grows with depth, \(p = p_0 + \rho gh\), so the upward push on the bottom face is larger than the downward push on the top face. The difference is \(F_\text{B}\).

# ============================ 14.5 FLUID DYNAMICS ============================

## QUIZ 14.5
Q: Water flows steadily through a pipe that narrows. In the narrow part, the water
A) moves faster
B) moves slower
C) moves at the same speed
D) stops
Answer: A
Why: The flow rate \(Q = Av\) is the same everywhere in the pipe, so a smaller area needs a larger speed: \(A_1v_1 = A_2v_2\).

## QUIZ 14.5

# ============================ 14.6 BERNOULLI'S EQUATION ============================

## QUIZ 14.6
Q: Water flows through a level pipe that narrows. How does the pressure in the narrow part compare to the wide part?
A) Lower
B) Higher
C) The same
D) It depends on the water's density
Answer: A
Why: The water moves faster in the narrow part. At the same height, Bernoulli's equation says faster flow goes with lower pressure.

## QUIZ 14.6
Q: Why does a shower curtain bulge inward when the shower is on?
A) The moving air and water inside have lower pressure than the still air outside
B) The water pulls the curtain with friction
C) The hot water makes the curtain lighter
D) The curtain is attracted to the water by gravity
Answer: A
Why: The spray drags air along with it. Faster moving air has lower pressure (Bernoulli's principle), so the still air outside pushes the curtain in.

## QUIZ 14.6
Q: Water flows out of a small hole a depth \(h\) below the open surface of a large tank. How fast does it come out?

C) Faster for a bigger tank
D) At \(gh\)
Answer: A
Why: Torricelli's theorem: \(v = \sqrt{2gh}\), the same as free fall through \(h\). The density cancels out of Bernoulli's equation.

## QUIZ 14.6
Q: Bernoulli's equation comes from which law?
A) Conservation of energy
B) Conservation of momentum
C) Newton's third law
D) Conservation of angular momentum
Answer: A
Why: Each term is an energy per volume: \(p\) is work done by pressure, \(\frac12\rho v^2\) is kinetic energy per volume, and \(\rho gy\) is potential energy per volume.

## QUIZ 14.6

C) They don't move
D) They spin
Answer: A
Why: The fast air between the balls has lower pressure than the still air on their outer sides, so they are pushed together.
