## SUMMARY 5.1
In short: A force is a push or a pull, a vector measured in newtons (N), where \(1\ \text{N} = 1\ \text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}^2\). Dynamics asks why objects move the way they do, while kinematics only describes the motion. The net force on an object is the vector sum of all the external forces acting on it, and a free body diagram shows those forces and nothing else.
Watch out: Only forces acting on the object go in its free body diagram, not forces the object exerts on other things.

## SUMMARY 5.2
In short: Newton's first law, the law of inertia: an object stays at rest or keeps moving at constant velocity unless a net external force acts on it. Mass measures inertia, how hard it is to change an object's motion. When the net force is zero, the object is in equilibrium. The law holds in inertial frames, frames that are not accelerating.
Watch out: Motion doesn't need a force. A change in motion does. A puck sliding at constant velocity on ice has zero net force.

## SUMMARY 5.3
In short: Newton's second law: \(\vec F_{net} = m\vec a\). The acceleration points in the direction of the net force and is inversely proportional to the mass. Apply it one component at a time, \(\sum F_x = ma_x\) and \(\sum F_y = ma_y\). In terms of momentum \(\vec p = m\vec v\), the net force is the rate of change of momentum, \(\vec F_{net} = d\vec p/dt\).
Watch out: Use the net force, the sum of all forces, not just the largest one. Only external forces count.

## SUMMARY 5.4
In short: Mass is the amount of matter and is the same everywhere. Weight is the gravitational force on an object, \(w = mg\), and depends on where you are: on the Moon you weigh about one sixth as much. In free fall the only force is weight, so \(a = g\) regardless of mass.
Watch out: Kilograms measure mass, and newtons measure weight. A 60 kg person weighs about 600 N on Earth.

## SUMMARY 5.5
In short: Newton's third law: when object A pushes on B, B pushes back on A with a force equal in size and opposite in direction, \(\vec F_{AB} = -\vec F_{BA}\). The two forces act on different objects, so they never cancel each other. Choosing the system tells you which of these forces are external and which are internal.
Watch out: A third law pair never appears on the same free body diagram. Weight and the normal force on a book are not a third law pair.

## SUMMARY 5.6
In short: The normal force \(N\) pushes perpendicular to a surface; on a level surface with no vertical acceleration, \(N = mg\), and on an incline at angle \(\theta\), \(N = mg\cos\theta\) while \(mg\sin\theta\) pulls along the slope. Tension \(T\) pulls along a rope and equals \(mg\) for a hanging object at rest. A spring exerts \(F = -kx\), opposite to its stretch or compression. Friction acts along a surface and opposes sliding.
Watch out: The normal force is not always equal to \(mg\). Check the angle and whether there's vertical acceleration or another vertical force.

## SUMMARY 5.7
In short: To draw a free body diagram, draw the object as a dot or box, then draw every force acting on it as an arrow starting at the object, each labeled. Choose axes, ideally with one along the acceleration, and split each force into components. Draw a separate diagram for each object in a problem.
Watch out: Don't draw "the force of motion" or \(m\vec a\) as a force. Every force must come from something touching the object, or from gravity.

## EQUATIONS 5.3

* Second law in components: \(\begin{aligned} \textstyle\sum F_x &= ma_x \\ \textstyle\sum F_y &= ma_y \\ \textstyle\sum F_z &= ma_z \end{aligned}\)
Second law, scalar form: \(F_{\text{net}} = ma\)
Second law, momentum form: \(\vec{F}_{\text{net}} = \frac{d\vec{p}}{dt}\)

# ============================ 5.1 FORCES ============================

## QUIZ 5.1
Q: What is one newton in base units?
A) \(\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}\)
B) \(\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}^2\)
C) \(\text{kg/s}^2\)
D) \(\text{m/s}^2\)
Answer: B
Why: From \(F = ma\), a newton is the force that gives 1 kg an acceleration of \(1\ \text{m/s}^2\).

## QUIZ 5.1
Q: Which question belongs to dynamics rather than kinematics?
A) How far does a car go in 5 s at constant speed?
B) What is a ball's velocity at the top of its flight?
C) Why does a sled slow down on snow?
D) How long does a dropped stone take to fall 20 m?
Answer: C
Why: Dynamics explains motion in terms of forces. The others only describe the motion.

## QUIZ 5.1
Q: What belongs on a free body diagram of a book?
A) Every force the book exerts on other objects
B) Every external force acting on the book
C) Only the book's weight
D) The book's velocity
Answer: B
Why: A free body diagram shows only the forces acting on the object, from outside it.

## QUIZ 5.1
Q: Two people push a box with 50 N each. What is the net force?
A) Always 100 N
B) Always 0
C) Anywhere from 0 to 100 N, depending on the directions
D) 50 N
Answer: C
Why: Forces are vectors. Pushing the same way gives 100 N, pushing opposite ways gives 0, and anything in between is possible.

# ============================ 5.2 FIRST LAW ============================

## QUIZ 5.2
Q: A hockey puck slides across frictionless ice at constant velocity. What is the net force on it?
A) In the direction of motion
B) Opposite the motion
C) Zero
D) Equal to its weight
Answer: C
Why: Constant velocity means no acceleration, so the net force is zero. No force is needed to keep it moving.

## QUIZ 5.2
Q: The bus you're standing on brakes suddenly, and you lurch forward. Why?
A) A forward force pushes you
B) Your body tends to keep moving at the bus's old velocity
C) Gravity pulls you forward
D) The floor pushes you forward
Answer: B
Why: That's inertia. The bus slows down, but nothing slows you as quickly, so you keep moving forward relative to the bus.

## QUIZ 5.2
Q: Which has more inertia?
A) A 2 kg ball
B) A 10 kg ball
C) A fast moving 1 kg ball
D) They all have the same
Answer: B
Why: Inertia depends only on mass. Speed doesn't change how hard it is to change an object's motion.

## QUIZ 5.2
Q: A car drives at a constant 25 m/s along a straight, level road. Which is true?
A) The engine's forward force is larger than friction and drag
B) The net force is zero
C) The car is not in equilibrium
D) There are no forces on the car
Answer: B
Why: Constant velocity means equilibrium. The forward push from the road balances the drag and friction.

## QUIZ 5.2
Q: Which frame of reference is inertial, to a good approximation?
A) A car turning a corner
B) An elevator speeding up
C) A train moving at constant velocity on a straight track
D) A merry go round
Answer: C
Why: An inertial frame is not accelerating. Turning, speeding up, or spinning frames are accelerating.

# ============================ 5.3 SECOND LAW ============================

## QUIZ 5.3
Q: The same net force acts on two carts. Cart B has twice the mass of cart A. How do their accelerations compare?
A) B's is twice A's
B) B's is half A's
C) They are equal
D) B's is four times A's
Answer: B
Why: \(a = F_{net}/m\), so doubling the mass halves the acceleration.

## QUIZ 5.3
Q: Which way does an object's acceleration point?
A) Along its velocity
B) Along the net force
C) Along the largest force
D) Opposite its velocity
Answer: B
Why: \(\vec F_{net} = m\vec a\), so the acceleration is always in the direction of the net force, whichever way the object moves.

## QUIZ 5.3
Q: A ball thrown straight up is at the top of its path. What is the net force on it?
A) Zero
B) Its weight, downward
C) Upward
D) Its weight plus the throwing force
Answer: B
Why: Once it leaves your hand, only gravity acts (ignoring air). The throwing force ended at release.

## QUIZ 5.3
Q: In \(\vec F_{net} = d\vec p/dt\), what is \(\vec p\)?
A) Power
B) Pressure
C) Momentum, \(m\vec v\)
D) Position
Answer: C
Why: Momentum is mass times velocity. The net force equals how fast the momentum changes.

# ============================ 5.4 MASS AND WEIGHT ============================

## QUIZ 5.4
Q: An astronaut with a mass of 80 kg goes from Earth to the Moon. What changes?
A) Her mass
B) Her weight
C) Both
D) Neither
Answer: B
Why: Mass is the amount of matter and doesn't change. Weight is \(mg\), and \(g\) on the Moon is about one sixth as large.

## QUIZ 5.4
Q: Ignoring air resistance, a 1 kg rock and a 10 kg rock are dropped. How do their accelerations compare?
A) The 10 kg rock's is 10 times larger
B) They are the same
C) The 1 kg rock's is larger
D) It depends on the height
Answer: B
Why: The heavier rock has 10 times the weight but also 10 times the mass, so \(a = w/m = g\) for both.

## QUIZ 5.4
Q: What does a bathroom scale actually measure?
A) Your mass
B) The normal force it pushes up on you with
C) Your volume
D) The force of gravity on the scale
Answer: B
Why: A scale reads the force it supports. When you stand still, that equals your weight. In an accelerating elevator it doesn't.

## QUIZ 5.4
Q: Astronauts in the space station float. Why?
A) There is no gravity in orbit
B) They and the station are both in free fall around Earth
C) The station's walls block gravity
D) They have no mass in space
Answer: B
Why: Gravity at the station is about 90% of its strength on the ground. Everything is falling together around Earth, so nothing presses on the floor.

# ============================ 5.5 THIRD LAW ============================

## QUIZ 5.5
Q: A heavy truck hits a small car. How do the forces they exert on each other compare?
A) The truck pushes harder
B) The car pushes harder
C) They are equal in size
D) It depends on their speeds
Answer: C
Why: By the third law the forces are equal and opposite. The car's acceleration is larger because its mass is smaller.

## QUIZ 5.5
Q: A book rests on a table. What is the third law partner of the book's weight (Earth pulling down on the book)?
A) The table pushing up on the book
B) The book pulling up on Earth
C) The book pushing down on the table
D) The table's weight
Answer: B
Why: Third law partners are the same type of force between the same two objects. Earth pulls the book, so the book pulls Earth.

## QUIZ 5.5
Q: If every force has an equal and opposite partner, how can anything accelerate?
A) The partners cancel only sometimes
B) The partners act on different objects, so they don't cancel on either one
C) Only the larger force counts
D) The third law only applies at rest
Answer: B
Why: Each force in a pair acts on a different object. To find an object's acceleration, add only the forces acting on it.

## QUIZ 5.5
Q: How does a swimmer push off a wall to move forward?
A) The water pushes her forward
B) She pushes back on the wall, and the wall pushes forward on her
C) Her muscles push her forward directly
D) Gravity pushes her forward
Answer: B
Why: She exerts a backward force on the wall, and by the third law the wall exerts an equal forward force on her. That external force accelerates her.

## DRILL 5.5
Q: You push on a wall with a force of \({{F}}\ \text{N}\). How large is the force the wall exerts on you?
Let: F = 50 to 300 by 50
Answer: F
Wrong: 0 | Walls push back. That's what keeps your hand from going through it.
Wrong: 2*F | Third law forces are equal in size.

# ============================ 5.6 COMMON FORCES ============================

## QUIZ 5.6
Q: A box sits on a ramp tilted at angle \(\theta\). What is the normal force on it?
A) \(mg\)
B) \(mg\cos\theta\)
C) \(mg\sin\theta\)
D) \(mg\tan\theta\)
Answer: B
Why: The normal force balances the part of the weight perpendicular to the ramp, \(mg\cos\theta\). The part along the ramp is \(mg\sin\theta\).

## QUIZ 5.6
Q: Which way does the normal force point?
A) Always straight up
B) Perpendicular to the surface, away from it
C) Along the surface
D) Straight down
Answer: B
Why: Normal means perpendicular. The surface pushes out from itself on whatever presses on it.

## QUIZ 5.6
Q: A spring is stretched to the right. Which way does it pull on the object attached to it?
A) Right
B) Left
C) Up
D) It doesn't pull
Answer: B
Why: \(F = -kx\). The spring force always points back toward the relaxed position.

## QUIZ 5.6
Q: A lamp hangs at rest from a single cord. What is the tension in the cord?
A) Zero
B) Equal to the lamp's weight
C) Half the lamp's weight
D) Twice the lamp's weight
Answer: B
Why: The lamp is in equilibrium, so the upward tension balances its weight: \(T = mg\).

## QUIZ 5.6
Q: As a ramp is tilted steeper, what happens to the normal force on a box resting on it?
A) It increases
B) It decreases
C) It stays the same
D) It becomes larger than the weight
Answer: B
Why: \(N = mg\cos\theta\), and \(\cos\theta\) shrinks as \(\theta\) grows.

Wrong: m/k | The spring holds the weight \(mg\), not the mass.
Wrong: m*10*k | Divide by \(k\).
Unit: m
Work: kx = mg\ \Rightarrow\ x = \frac{({{m}})(10)}{{{k}}} = {{answer}}\ \text{m}


# ============================ 5.7 FREE BODY DIAGRAMS ============================

## QUIZ 5.7
Q: A ball flies through the air after being kicked. Ignoring air resistance, which forces belong on its free body diagram?
A) Weight and the kick
B) Weight only
C) Weight and a forward force of motion
D) No forces
Answer: B
Why: The kick ended when the foot left the ball. In the air, only gravity acts.

## QUIZ 5.7
Q: A box is pulled across a rough floor by a rope at an angle above horizontal. How many forces act on the box?
A) 2
B) 3
C) 4
D) 5
Answer: C
Why: Weight, the normal force, the rope's tension, and kinetic friction.

## QUIZ 5.7
Q: Two blocks are in contact, and you push the first one. On the free body diagram of the second block, what horizontal force appears?
A) Your push
B) The push from the first block
C) Both
D) None
Answer: B
Why: You touch only the first block. The second block is pushed by the first block, so that's the only horizontal force on it, if the floor is frictionless.

## QUIZ 5.7
Q: Why is it often helpful to tilt the axes along a ramp?
A) It changes the forces
B) The acceleration then lies along one axis, so only one equation has \(ma\)
C) Gravity points along the ramp
D) The normal force disappears
Answer: B
Why: With \(x\) along the ramp, \(a_y = 0\). Only the weight needs to be split into components.

## DRILL 5.7
Q: You push two blocks in contact across a frictionless floor with \({{F}}\ \text{N}\). Block 1 (\({{m1}}\ \text{kg}\)) is in back and block 2 (\({{m2}}\ \text{kg}\)) in front. What is their acceleration?
Let: m1 = 1 to 5 by 1; m2 = 1 to 5 by 1; k = 1 to 4 by 1; F = (m1 + m2)*k

Wrong: F/m2 | Both blocks accelerate together. Use the total mass.
Wrong: F*(m1 + m2) | Divide by the total mass.

## DRILL 5.7

## DRILL 5.7
Q: A block with a mass of \({{m}}\ \text{kg}\) on a level table is pulled sideways by a horizontal rope. There is no vertical acceleration. What is the normal force on the block? Use \(g = 10\ \text{m/s}^2\).
