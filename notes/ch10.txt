## SUMMARY 10.1
In short: Rotation about a fixed axis uses angles instead of distances. The angle \(\theta\) in radians is arc length over radius, \(\theta = \frac{s}{r}\), so one full turn is \(2\pi\) rad. Angular velocity \(\omega = \frac{d\theta}{dt}\) (rad/s) says how fast the angle changes, and angular acceleration \(\alpha = \frac{d\omega}{dt}\) (rad/s²) says how fast \(\omega\) changes. Every point on a rigid body has the same \(\theta\), \(\omega\), and \(\alpha\), but points farther out move faster: \(v_\text{t} = r\omega\) and \(a_\text{t} = r\alpha\). Counterclockwise is positive by convention.
Watch out: Use radians in \(v_\text{t} = r\omega\) and \(s = r\theta\). Turn rpm into rad/s first: multiply by \(2\pi\) and divide by 60.

## SUMMARY 10.2
In short: With constant angular acceleration, rotation follows the same equations as straight line motion with constant acceleration, with \(\theta\) for \(x\), \(\omega\) for \(v\), and \(\alpha\) for \(a\): \(\omega_\text{f} = \omega_0 + \alpha t\), \(\theta_\text{f} = \theta_0 + \omega_0t + \frac12\alpha t^2\), and \(\omega_\text{f}^2 = \omega_0^2 + 2\alpha\Delta\theta\). The average angular velocity is \(\bar\omega = \frac{\omega_0 + \omega_\text{f}}{2}\). Pick the equation that leaves out the quantity you don't know and don't need.
Watch out: A wheel slowing down has \(\alpha\) opposite to \(\omega\). If \(\omega\) is positive, a slowing wheel has negative \(\alpha\).

## SUMMARY 10.3
In short: A point at distance \(r\) from the axis moves on a circle, so its speed is \(v_\text{t} = r\omega\) and its centripetal acceleration is \(a_\text{c} = \frac{v_\text{t}^2}{r} = r\omega^2\), pointing toward the axis. If the rotation speeds up or slows down there is also a tangential acceleration \(a_\text{t} = r\alpha\) along the path. The two are at right angles, so the total acceleration has size \(\sqrt{a_\text{c}^2 + a_\text{t}^2}\). A wheel rolling without slipping moves forward at \(v = r\omega\).
Watch out: Even at constant \(\omega\) every point off the axis accelerates, because its direction keeps changing. Only \(a_\text{t}\) is zero then.

## SUMMARY 10.4
In short: A spinning object has rotational kinetic energy \(K = \frac12I\omega^2\), where the moment of inertia \(I = \sum m_jr_j^2\) (kg·m²) measures how hard it is to change its rotation. \(I\) depends on the mass and on how far that mass sits from the axis: mass far out counts much more, because \(r\) is squared. The same object has different \(I\) about different axes. Energy is conserved as usual; a rolling or spinning object just has this extra kind of kinetic energy.
Watch out: \(I\) is not just mass. A hoop and a disk of the same mass and radius have different \(I\), because the hoop's mass is all at the rim.

## SUMMARY 10.5
In short: For a solid object, \(I = \int r^2\,dm\). The results for common shapes go in a table: a thin hoop about its center \(MR^2\), a solid disk or cylinder \(\frac12MR^2\), a solid sphere \(\frac25MR^2\), a thin rod about its center \(\frac1{12}ML^2\) and about one end \(\frac13ML^2\). The parallel axis theorem moves the axis: \(I = I_\text{CM} + Md^2\), where \(d\) is the distance from the center of mass. For an object made of parts, add the parts' moments of inertia about the same axis.
Watch out: The parallel axis theorem only works starting from the axis through the center of mass, and the moment of inertia is smallest there.

## SUMMARY 10.6
In short: Torque is the turning effect of a force: \(\tau = r_\perp F = rF\sin\theta\) (N·m), where \(r\) is the distance from the axis to where the force acts and \(\theta\) is the angle between \(\vec r\) and \(\vec F\). A force through the axis, or along the line toward it, gives no torque. Pushing farther from the axis or at a right angle gives more torque, which is why doorknobs sit far from the hinges. As a vector, \(\vec\tau = \vec r\times\vec F\); counterclockwise torques are positive. The net torque is the sum of all the torques.
Watch out: Torque depends on where the force acts, not just how big it is. The same force can give a large torque, a small torque, or none.

## SUMMARY 10.7
In short: Newton's second law for rotation is \(\sum\tau = I\alpha\): the net torque sets the angular acceleration, and the moment of inertia plays the role of mass. A bigger torque spins things up faster; a bigger \(I\) resists. For a mass hanging from a rope on a pulley, write \(F = ma\) for the mass and \(\tau = I\alpha\) for the pulley, linked by \(a = r\alpha\). The rope tension is less than the hanging weight when the mass speeds up downward.
Watch out: Use the net torque, with signs. Two torques that turn opposite ways partly cancel.

## SUMMARY 10.8
In short: A torque does work as it turns an object through an angle: \(W = \tau\,\Delta\theta\) for a constant torque (in joules, with \(\theta\) in radians). The work energy theorem holds for rotation: the net work equals the change in rotational kinetic energy, \(W = \frac12I\omega_\text{f}^2 - \frac12I\omega_0^2\). The power delivered by a torque is \(P = \tau\omega\), the rotational version of \(P = Fv\). An engine with a given power gives more torque at low rotation speed.
Watch out: Angles must be in radians in \(W = \tau\Delta\theta\) and \(P = \tau\omega\). One turn is \(2\pi\) rad, not 360.

## EQUATIONS 10.1
* Angular position: \(\theta = \dfrac{s}{r}\) | The angle \(\theta\) in radians is arc length \(s\) (m) over radius \(r\) (m). One full turn is \(2\pi\) rad.
* Angular velocity: \(\omega = \dfrac{d\theta}{dt}\) | Angular velocity \(\omega\) (rad/s) is how fast the angle changes. Every point of a rigid body has the same \(\omega\).
* Angular acceleration: \(\alpha = \dfrac{d\omega}{dt}\) | Angular acceleration \(\alpha\) (rad/s²) is how fast the angular velocity changes.
* Tangential speed: \(v_\text{t} = r\omega\) | A point \(r\) (m) from the axis moves at \(v_\text{t}\) (m/s) along its circle. Use \(\omega\) in rad/s.
Tangential acceleration: \(a_\text{t} = r\alpha\) | The acceleration along the path (m/s²) of a point \(r\) from the axis when the rotation speeds up or slows down.
Period and frequency: \(\omega = \dfrac{2\pi}{T} = 2\pi f\) | One turn takes the period \(T\) (s); \(f\) (Hz) is turns per second. Multiply rpm by \(2\pi/60\) to get rad/s.

## EQUATIONS 10.2
* Angular velocity, constant α: \(\omega_\text{f} = \omega_0 + \alpha t\) | Final angular velocity (rad/s) after time \(t\) (s) at constant angular acceleration \(\alpha\) (rad/s²).
* Angle, constant α: \(\theta_\text{f} = \theta_0 + \omega_0t + \tfrac12\alpha t^2\) | The angle turned (rad) at constant \(\alpha\). Same form as \(x = x_0 + v_0t + \tfrac12at^2\).
* Without time: \(\omega_\text{f}^2 = \omega_0^2 + 2\alpha\,\Delta\theta\) | Links speeds and angle turned (rad) when the time isn't given or needed.
Average angular velocity: \(\bar\omega = \dfrac{\omega_0 + \omega_\text{f}}{2}\) | Only for constant \(\alpha\). Then the angle turned is \(\Delta\theta = \bar\omega t\).

## EQUATIONS 10.3
* Centripetal acceleration: \(a_\text{c} = \dfrac{v_\text{t}^2}{r} = r\omega^2\) | A point \(r\) (m) from the axis accelerates toward the axis at \(a_\text{c}\) (m/s²), even at constant \(\omega\).
Total acceleration: \(a = \sqrt{a_\text{c}^2 + a_\text{t}^2}\) | The centripetal part points to the axis and the tangential part along the path, at right angles, so they add like the sides of a right triangle.
Rolling without slipping: \(v = r\omega\) | A wheel of radius \(r\) (m) rolling without slipping moves forward at \(v\) (m/s).

## EQUATIONS 10.4
* Moment of inertia: \(I = \sum_j m_jr_j^2\) | \(I\) (kg·m²) adds each mass (kg) times its distance from the axis squared (m²). Mass far from the axis counts most.
* Rotational kinetic energy: \(K = \tfrac12I\omega^2\) | Kinetic energy (J) of a spinning object with moment of inertia \(I\) (kg·m²) and angular velocity \(\omega\) (rad/s).

## EQUATIONS 10.5
Moment of inertia, continuous object: \(I = \int r^2\,dm\) | For a solid object, add \(r^2\) times each small piece of mass \(dm\). Shape tables give the results.
* Common shapes: \(\begin{aligned}&\text{hoop } MR^2,\ \text{disk } \tfrac12MR^2\\ &\text{solid sphere } \tfrac25MR^2\\ &\text{rod about center } \tfrac1{12}ML^2\\ &\text{rod about end } \tfrac13ML^2\end{aligned}\) | Each with mass \(M\) (kg), radius \(R\) or length \(L\) (m), about the axis named. Mass farther out gives a bigger number in front.
* Parallel axis theorem: \(I = I_\text{CM} + Md^2\) | Moment of inertia about an axis \(d\) (m) from a parallel axis through the center of mass. It is always bigger than \(I_\text{CM}\).
Compound object: \(I_\text{total} = \sum_i I_i\) | Add the moments of inertia of the parts, all about the same axis.

## EQUATIONS 10.6
* Torque: \(\tau = r_\perp F = rF\sin\theta\) | Torque \(\tau\) (N·m) from force \(F\) (N) acting \(r\) (m) from the axis at angle \(\theta\) to \(\vec r\). \(r_\perp\) is the lever arm.
Torque vector: \(\vec\tau = \vec r\times\vec F\) | Torque points along the axis by the right hand rule. Counterclockwise torques are positive.
Net torque: \(\tau_\text{net} = \sum_i \tau_i\) | Add all the torques with their signs.

## EQUATIONS 10.7
* Newton's second law for rotation: \(\sum\tau = I\alpha\) | Net torque (N·m) equals moment of inertia (kg·m²) times angular acceleration (rad/s²). \(I\) plays the role of mass.

## EQUATIONS 10.8
* Work by a constant torque: \(W = \tau\,\Delta\theta\) | Work (J) done by torque \(\tau\) (N·m) turning through \(\Delta\theta\) (rad).
Work by a varying torque: \(W = \int_{\theta_A}^{\theta_B}\tau\,d\theta\) | Add torque times each small angle. It is the area under a torque against angle graph.
* Work energy theorem for rotation: \(W = \tfrac12I\omega_\text{f}^2 - \tfrac12I\omega_0^2\) | The net work (J) done on a spinning object equals its change in rotational kinetic energy.
* Rotational power: \(P = \tau\omega\) | Power (W) from torque \(\tau\) (N·m) at angular velocity \(\omega\) (rad/s). The rotational version of \(P = Fv\).

## QUIZ 10.1
Q: How many radians are in one full turn?
A) \(2\pi\)
B) \(\pi\)
C) \(360\)
D) \(1\)
Answer: A
Why: The circumference is \(2\pi r\), so a full turn has \(\theta = \frac{s}{r} = \frac{2\pi r}{r} = 2\pi\) rad.

## QUIZ 10.1
Q: A merry go round spins at a steady rate. A child sits near the edge and another near the center. Which is true?
A) Same angular velocity; the outer child has a larger speed
B) Same speed; the outer child has a larger angular velocity
C) Same angular velocity and same speed
D) The inner child has the larger speed
Answer: A
Why: Every point turns through the same angle in the same time, so \(\omega\) is the same. But \(v_\text{t} = r\omega\), so the child farther out moves faster.

## QUIZ 10.1
Q: A wheel turns counterclockwise and is slowing down. What are the signs of \(\omega\) and \(\alpha\)?
A) \(\omega\) positive, \(\alpha\) negative
B) Both positive
C) Both negative
D) \(\omega\) negative, \(\alpha\) positive
Answer: A
Why: Counterclockwise is positive, so \(\omega > 0\). Slowing down means \(\alpha\) points opposite to \(\omega\), so \(\alpha < 0\).

## QUIZ 10.1
Q: Why must \(\omega\) be in rad/s, not rpm or degrees per second, in \(v_\text{t} = r\omega\)?
A) The relation comes from \(s = r\theta\), which only works with \(\theta\) in radians
B) Radians are an SI base unit
C) It doesn't matter; any angle unit works
D) Degrees are too small
Answer: A
Why: \(\theta = s/r\) defines the radian. Taking the time rate of \(s = r\theta\) gives \(v_\text{t} = r\omega\), so the radian is built in.

# ============================ 10.2 ROTATION WITH CONSTANT ANGULAR ACCELERATION ============================

## QUIZ 10.2
Q: Which straight line equation matches \(\omega_\text{f} = \omega_0 + \alpha t\)?
A) \(v = v_0 + at\)
B) \(x = x_0 + vt\)
C) \(v^2 = v_0^2 + 2a\Delta x\)
D) \(F = ma\)
Answer: A
Why: Swap \(\omega\) for \(v\) and \(\alpha\) for \(a\). Every constant acceleration equation has a rotational twin.

## QUIZ 10.2
Q: A wheel starts from rest with constant \(\alpha\). In the first 2 s it turns through 3 rad. How far does it turn in the first 4 s?
A) 12 rad
B) 6 rad
C) 9 rad
D) 24 rad
Answer: A
Why: From rest, \(\theta = \frac12\alpha t^2\) grows with \(t^2\). Doubling the time gives 4 times the angle: \(4 \times 3 = 12\) rad.

## QUIZ 10.2
Q: A spinning wheel slows to a stop. You know \(\omega_0\), \(\alpha\), and want the angle it turns. You don't know the time. Which equation fits best?
A) \(\omega_\text{f}^2 = \omega_0^2 + 2\alpha\Delta\theta\)
B) \(\omega_\text{f} = \omega_0 + \alpha t\)
C) \(\theta_\text{f} = \theta_0 + \omega_0t + \frac12\alpha t^2\)
D) \(\omega = \frac{2\pi}{T}\)
Answer: A
Why: With \(\omega_\text{f} = 0\), this one has \(\Delta\theta\) as its only unknown and no \(t\) at all.

## QUIZ 10.2
Q: When can you use \(\bar\omega = \frac{\omega_0 + \omega_\text{f}}{2}\) for the average angular velocity?
A) Only when the angular acceleration is constant
B) Always
C) Only when the wheel starts from rest
D) Only when \(\omega\) is constant
Answer: A
Why: The average of the start and end values equals the time average only when \(\omega\) changes at a steady rate.

Q: A wheel spinning at \({{w0}}\ \text{rad/s}\) slows steadily at \({{a}}\ \text{rad/s}^2\) until it stops. Through what angle does it turn while stopping?

## DRILL 10.2
Q: A grinding wheel goes from \({{w0}}\) to \({{wf}}\ \text{rad/s}\) at a steady rate in \({{t}}\ \text{s}\). Through what angle does it turn?

D) 2 rad
Answer: A
Why: The angle is the area under the \(\omega\) against \(t\) graph, a triangle: \(\frac12(4)(8) = 16\) rad. Choice B is the final angular velocity.


# ============================ 10.3 RELATING ANGULAR AND TRANSLATIONAL QUANTITIES ============================

## QUIZ 10.3
Q: A wheel spins at a constant angular velocity. Does a point on its rim accelerate?
A) Yes, toward the axis
B) No, because \(\omega\) is constant
C) Yes, along its path
D) Yes, away from the axis
Answer: A
Why: The point moves in a circle, so its direction changes. It has centripetal acceleration \(r\omega^2\) toward the axis. With \(\alpha = 0\), there is no tangential part.

## QUIZ 10.3
Q: A carousel speeds up. Which way does a rider's total acceleration point?
A) Partly toward the center and partly forward along the path
B) Straight toward the center
C) Straight forward along the path
D) Straight outward
Answer: A
Why: Moving in a circle gives \(a_\text{c}\) toward the center; speeding up adds \(a_\text{t}\) along the path. The total is the sum of these two perpendicular parts.

## QUIZ 10.3
Q: Point B on a wheel is twice as far from the axis as point A. How does B's centripetal acceleration compare with A's?
A) Twice as big
B) The same
C) Four times as big
D) Half as big
Answer: A
Why: Both have the same \(\omega\), and \(a_\text{c} = r\omega^2\) grows in step with \(r\).

## DRILL 10.3
Q: A point \({{r}}\ \text{m}\) from the axis of a wheel turning at \({{w}}\ \text{rad/s}\). What is its centripetal acceleration?
Let: r = one of 0.1, 0.2, 0.5; w = one of 4, 10, 20
Answer: r*w^2
Wrong: r*w | That is the speed, \(v_\text{t} = r\omega\).
Wrong: w^2/r | With \(\omega\) it is \(a_\text{c} = r\omega^2\); the form \(v^2/r\) uses the speed.
Wrong: r^2*w | Square \(\omega\), not \(r\): \(a_\text{c} = r\omega^2\).
Unit: m/s²
Work: a_\text{c} = r\omega^2 = ({{r}})({{w}})^2 = {{answer}}\ \text{m/s}^2

## DRILL 10.3
Q: A bike wheel of radius \({{r}}\ \text{m}\) rolls without slipping at \({{v}}\ \text{m/s}\). What is its angular velocity?
Let: r = one of 0.25, 0.3, 0.4, 0.5; v = one of 3, 5, 6, 10
Answer: v/r
Wrong: v*r | Rolling means \(v = r\omega\), so \(\omega = v/r\).
Wrong: v/(2*pi*r) | That is turns per second. In rad/s, \(\omega = v/r\).
Wrong: r/v | Flip it: \(\omega = v/r\).
Unit: rad/s
Work: \omega = \frac{v}{r} = \frac{{{v}}}{{{r}}} = {{answer}}\ \text{rad/s}

## DRILL 10.3
Q: A point on a wheel has centripetal acceleration \({{ac}}\ \text{m/s}^2\) and tangential acceleration \({{at}}\ \text{m/s}^2\). What is the size of its total acceleration?
Let: k = one of 1, 2, 4; ac = 4*k; at = 3*k
Answer: sqrt(ac^2 + at^2)
Wrong: ac + at | The two parts are at right angles, so use the Pythagorean theorem.
Wrong: ac - at | They don't cancel; they are perpendicular.
Wrong: ac | Include the tangential part too.
Unit: m/s²
Work: a = \sqrt{a_\text{c}^2 + a_\text{t}^2} = \sqrt{({{ac}})^2 + ({{at}})^2} = {{answer}}\ \text{m/s}^2


# ============================ 10.4 MOMENT OF INERTIA AND ROTATIONAL KINETIC ENERGY ============================

## QUIZ 10.4
Q: A hoop and a solid disk have the same mass and radius. Which has the larger moment of inertia about its center?
A) The hoop
B) The disk
C) They are the same
D) It depends on how fast they spin
Answer: A
Why: All of the hoop's mass is at the rim, at the largest \(r\). The disk has mass closer in, and \(I = \sum mr^2\) favors mass far out.

## QUIZ 10.4
Q: A spinning wheel's angular velocity doubles. What happens to its rotational kinetic energy?
A) It becomes 4 times as large
B) It doubles
C) It stays the same
D) It becomes 8 times as large
Answer: A
Why: \(K = \frac12I\omega^2\) grows with \(\omega^2\). Twice the \(\omega\) gives \(2^2 = 4\) times the energy.

## QUIZ 10.4
Q: What is the unit of moment of inertia?
A) \(\text{kg}\cdot\text{m}^2\)
B) \(\text{kg}\cdot\text{m}\)
C) \(\text{N}\cdot\text{m}\)
D) \(\text{kg}\cdot\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2\)
Answer: A
Why: \(I = \sum mr^2\) is mass times distance squared. Choice D is a joule.

## QUIZ 10.4
Q: A thin rod spins once about its center and once about one end, at the same \(\omega\). When does it have more kinetic energy?
A) About the end
B) About the center
C) The same both ways
D) It can't spin about an end
Answer: A
Why: About an end, more of the mass is far from the axis, so \(I\) is bigger, and \(K = \frac12I\omega^2\) is bigger at the same \(\omega\).

## DRILL 10.4
Q: Two \({{m}}\ \text{kg}\) balls sit at the ends of a light rod, each \({{r}}\ \text{m}\) from the center. What is the moment of inertia about the center?
Let: m = one of 1, 2, 3, 5; r = one of 0.2, 0.5, 1, 2
Answer: 2*m*r^2
Wrong: m*r^2 | There are two balls. Add \(mr^2\) for each.
Wrong: 2*m*r | Square the distance: \(I = \sum mr^2\).
Wrong: m*(2*r)^2 | Each ball is \(r\) from the axis, not \(2r\).
Unit: kg·m²
Work: I = \sum mr^2 = 2({{m}})({{r}})^2 = {{answer}}\ \text{kg}\cdot\text{m}^2

## DRILL 10.4
Q: A flywheel with moment of inertia \({{I}}\ \text{kg}\cdot\text{m}^2\) spins at \({{w}}\ \text{rad/s}\). What is its rotational kinetic energy?
Let: I = one of 0.5, 2, 4, 10; w = one of 10, 20, 30
Answer: 0.5*I*w^2
Wrong: I*w^2 | Don't forget the \(\tfrac12\): \(K = \tfrac12I\omega^2\).
Wrong: 0.5*I*w | Square the angular velocity.
Wrong: I*w | That is the angular momentum (Chapter 11), not the energy.
Unit: J
Work: K = \tfrac12I\omega^2 = \tfrac12({{I}})({{w}})^2 = {{answer}}\ \text{J}

## DRILL 10.4
Q: A wheel with moment of inertia \({{I}}\ \text{kg}\cdot\text{m}^2\) has \({{K}}\ \text{J}\) of rotational kinetic energy. How fast is it spinning?
Let: I = one of 2, 4, 8; w = one of 5, 10, 20; K = 0.5*I*w^2
Answer: sqrt(2*K/I)
Wrong: 2*K/I | Take the square root: \(\omega = \sqrt{2K/I}\).
Wrong: sqrt(K/I) | Keep the 2 from the \(\tfrac12\).
Wrong: sqrt(2*K*I) | Divide by \(I\), don't multiply.
Unit: rad/s
Work: K = \tfrac12I\omega^2 \Rightarrow \omega = \sqrt{\frac{2({{K}})}{{{I}}}} = {{answer}}\ \text{rad/s}


# ============================ 10.5 CALCULATING MOMENTS OF INERTIA ============================

## QUIZ 10.5
Q: Rank these by moment of inertia about their centers, largest first. All have the same mass and radius: hoop, solid disk, solid sphere.
A) Hoop, disk, sphere
B) Sphere, disk, hoop
C) Disk, hoop, sphere
D) All the same
Answer: A
Why: Hoop \(MR^2\), disk \(\frac12MR^2\), sphere \(\frac25MR^2\). The more mass near the axis, the smaller \(I\).

## QUIZ 10.5
Q: The parallel axis theorem \(I = I_\text{CM} + Md^2\) starts from which moment of inertia?
A) The one about a parallel axis through the center of mass
B) The one about any parallel axis
C) The largest possible one
D) The one about a perpendicular axis
Answer: A
Why: The theorem only works starting from the axis through the center of mass. That is also the axis with the smallest \(I\).

## QUIZ 10.5
Q: Why is a thin rod's moment of inertia about one end (\(\frac13ML^2\)) bigger than about its center (\(\frac1{12}ML^2\))?
A) About an end, much of the mass is farther from the axis
B) The rod is longer when spun about an end
C) Spinning about an end adds mass
D) It isn't; they are equal
Answer: A
Why: About the center no bit of the rod is more than \(L/2\) away; about an end some is a full \(L\) away, and \(r\) is squared.

## DRILL 10.5
Q: A solid disk has mass \({{M}}\ \text{kg}\) and radius \({{R}}\ \text{m}\). What is its moment of inertia about its center?
Let: M = one of 2, 4, 10, 20; R = one of 0.1, 0.2, 0.5
Answer: 0.5*M*R^2
Wrong: M*R^2 | That is a hoop. A solid disk has \(\tfrac12MR^2\).
Wrong: 0.4*M*R^2 | That is a solid sphere, \(\tfrac25MR^2\).
Wrong: 0.5*M*R | Square the radius.
Unit: kg·m²
Work: I = \tfrac12MR^2 = \tfrac12({{M}})({{R}})^2 = {{answer}}\ \text{kg}\cdot\text{m}^2

## DRILL 10.5
Q: A thin rod of mass \({{M}}\ \text{kg}\) and length \({{L}}\ \text{m}\) has \(I_\text{CM} = \tfrac1{12}ML^2\). Use the parallel axis theorem to find its moment of inertia about one end.
Let: M = one of 1, 2, 3, 6; L = one of 1, 2, 3
Answer: M*L^2/12 + M*(L/2)^2
Wrong: M*L^2/12 + M*L^2 | The end is \(L/2\) from the center of mass, not \(L\).
Wrong: M*L^2/12 | Add \(Md^2\) to move the axis to the end.
Wrong: M*(L/2)^2 | Start from \(I_\text{CM}\) and add \(Md^2\) to it.
Unit: kg·m²
Work: I = \tfrac1{12}ML^2 + M\left(\tfrac{L}{2}\right)^2 = \tfrac13ML^2 = {{answer}}\ \text{kg}\cdot\text{m}^2

## DRILL 10.5
Q: A \({{m}}\ \text{kg}\) solid sphere of radius \({{R}}\ \text{m}\) hangs at the end of a light string, its center \({{d}}\ \text{m}\) from the pivot. What is its moment of inertia about the pivot?
Let: m = one of 1, 2, 5; R = one of 0.1, 0.2; d = one of 0.5, 1, 2
Answer: 0.4*m*R^2 + m*d^2
Wrong: m*d^2 | That treats the sphere as a point. Add its own \(\tfrac25mR^2\) about its center.
Wrong: 0.4*m*R^2 | Add \(md^2\) to move the axis out to the pivot.
Wrong: 0.4*m*R^2 + m*d | Square the distance in \(md^2\).
Unit: kg·m²
Work: I = \tfrac25mR^2 + md^2 = \tfrac25({{m}})({{R}})^2 + ({{m}})({{d}})^2 = {{answer}}\ \text{kg}\cdot\text{m}^2


# ============================ 10.6 TORQUE ============================

## QUIZ 10.6
Q: Where should you push on a door to open it most easily?
A) At the edge farthest from the hinges, at a right angle to the door
B) Near the hinges, at a right angle to the door
C) At the far edge, pushing toward the hinges
D) Anywhere; only the size of the push matters
Answer: A
Why: \(\tau = rF\sin\theta\) is biggest with the largest \(r\) and \(\theta = 90^\circ\). Pushing toward the hinges has \(\theta = 0\), so no torque at all.

## QUIZ 10.6
Q: A force acts along a line that passes straight through the axis. What torque does it make?
A) Zero
B) The largest possible
C) \(rF\)
D) It depends on the force's size
Answer: A
Why: Its lever arm \(r_\perp\) is zero, so \(\tau = r_\perp F = 0\).

## QUIZ 10.6
Q: What is the unit of torque?
A) \(\text{N}\cdot\text{m}\)
B) \(\text{N/m}\)
C) \(\text{kg}\cdot\text{m}^2\)
D) \(\text{N}\cdot\text{s}\)
Answer: A
Why: Torque is force times lever arm. It has the same units as a joule, but it is not energy, so it is written N·m.

## QUIZ 10.6
Q: On a seesaw, a 30 kg child sits 2 m from the pivot. Where must a 20 kg child sit on the other side to balance it?
A) 3 m from the pivot
B) 2 m from the pivot
C) 1.33 m from the pivot
D) 4 m from the pivot
Answer: A
Why: The torques must cancel: \((30)(g)(2) = (20)(g)(r)\), so \(r = 3\ \text{m}\). The lighter child sits farther out.

## DRILL 10.6
Q: You push at a right angle on a wrench \({{r}}\ \text{m}\) from the bolt with a force of \({{F}}\ \text{N}\). What torque do you make?
Let: r = one of 0.2, 0.25, 0.3, 0.5; F = one of 20, 40, 50, 100
Answer: r*F
Wrong: F/r | Torque is force times lever arm: \(\tau = rF\).
Wrong: F | Multiply by the distance from the bolt.
Wrong: r*F/2 | At a right angle \(\sin 90^\circ = 1\), so \(\tau = rF\).
Unit: N·m
Work: \tau = rF\sin 90^\circ = ({{r}})({{F}}) = {{answer}}\ \text{N}\cdot\text{m}

## DRILL 10.6
Q: A force of \({{F}}\ \text{N}\) pulls \({{r}}\ \text{m}\) from an axis at \(30^\circ\) to the line from the axis \((\sin 30^\circ = 0.5)\). What torque does it make?
Let: F = one of 10, 20, 40, 60; r = one of 0.5, 1, 2
Answer: r*F*0.5
Wrong: r*F | Only the part of the force at a right angle to \(\vec r\) counts: multiply by \(\sin 30^\circ\).
Wrong: r*F*0.87 | That uses \(\cos 30^\circ\). Torque uses \(\sin\theta\) of the angle between \(\vec r\) and \(\vec F\).
Wrong: F*0.5/r | Multiply by the distance, don't divide.
Unit: N·m
Work: \tau = rF\sin\theta = ({{r}})({{F}})(0.5) = {{answer}}\ \text{N}\cdot\text{m}

## DRILL 10.6
Q: Two forces turn a wheel: one makes \({{t1}}\ \text{N}\cdot\text{m}\) counterclockwise, the other \({{t2}}\ \text{N}\cdot\text{m}\) clockwise. What is the net torque, taking counterclockwise as positive?
Let: t1 = one of 20, 30, 50; t2 = one of 5, 10, 15
Answer: t1 - t2
Wrong: t1 + t2 | Clockwise torque is negative, so subtract it.
Wrong: t2 - t1 | Counterclockwise is positive here, so the counterclockwise torque gets the plus sign.
Wrong: t1 | Include the clockwise torque too.
Unit: N·m
Work: \tau_\text{net} = {{t1}} - {{t2}} = {{answer}}\ \text{N}\cdot\text{m}


# ============================ 10.7 NEWTON'S SECOND LAW FOR ROTATION ============================

## QUIZ 10.7
Q: The same torque acts on a hoop and on a solid disk with the same mass and radius. Which gets the larger angular acceleration?
A) The disk
B) The hoop
C) They are the same
D) Neither one turns
Answer: A
Why: \(\alpha = \tau/I\). The disk has the smaller \(I\) (\(\frac12MR^2\) against \(MR^2\)), so it spins up faster.

## QUIZ 10.7
Q: In \(\sum\tau = I\alpha\), what plays the role that mass plays in \(F = ma\)?
A) The moment of inertia \(I\)
B) The torque
C) The radius
D) The angular velocity
Answer: A
Why: \(I\) measures how hard it is to change the rotation, just as mass measures how hard it is to change straight line motion.

## QUIZ 10.7
Q: A bucket hangs from a rope wound on a heavy pulley and is falling faster and faster. How does the rope tension compare with the bucket's weight?
A) Less than the weight
B) Equal to the weight
C) More than the weight
D) Zero
Answer: A
Why: The bucket speeds up downward, so the net force on it points down: \(mg - T = ma > 0\). The tension must also turn the heavy pulley, but it is still less than \(mg\).

## DRILL 10.7
Q: A net torque of \({{tau}}\ \text{N}\cdot\text{m}\) acts on a wheel with moment of inertia \({{I}}\ \text{kg}\cdot\text{m}^2\). What is its angular acceleration?
Let: I = one of 0.5, 2, 4, 5; tau = one of 10, 20, 40
Answer: tau/I
Wrong: tau*I | Divide the torque by the moment of inertia: \(\alpha = \tau/I\).
Wrong: I/tau | Flip it: \(\alpha = \tau/I\).
Wrong: tau/(0.5*I) | No factor of \(\tfrac12\) here. That belongs in the energy, not the second law.
Unit: rad/s²
Work: \alpha = \frac{\tau}{I} = \frac{{{tau}}}{{{I}}} = {{answer}}\ \text{rad/s}^2

## DRILL 10.7
Q: A rope pulls tangentially with \({{F}}\ \text{N}\) on the rim of a solid disk of mass \({{M}}\ \text{kg}\) and radius \({{R}}\ \text{m}\). What is the disk's angular acceleration?
Let: F = one of 10, 20, 30; M = one of 2, 4, 5, 10; R = one of 0.2, 0.5
Answer: F*R/(0.5*M*R^2)
Wrong: F*R/(0.5*M*R) | Square the radius in \(I = \tfrac12MR^2\).
Wrong: F*R/(M*R^2) | That treats the disk like a hoop. A solid disk has \(I = \tfrac12MR^2\).
Wrong: F/M | That is a straight line acceleration in m/s², not \(\alpha\).
Unit: rad/s²
Work: \alpha = \frac{FR}{\tfrac12MR^2} = \frac{2F}{MR} = \frac{2({{F}})}{({{M}})({{R}})} = {{answer}}\ \text{rad/s}^2

## DRILL 10.7
Q: A spinning wheel with moment of inertia \({{I}}\ \text{kg}\cdot\text{m}^2\) slows from \({{w}}\ \text{rad/s}\) to rest in \({{t}}\ \text{s}\). What size friction torque acts on it?
Let: I = one of 0.5, 1, 2, 4; w = one of 20, 30, 60; t = one of 5, 10, 15
Answer: I*w/t
Wrong: w/t | That is \(\alpha\). Multiply by \(I\) to get the torque.
Wrong: I*w*t | \(\alpha = \omega/t\); divide by the time.
Wrong: 0.5*I*w^2/t | That is the average power lost, in watts.
Unit: N·m
Work: \alpha = \frac{\omega}{t},\quad \tau = I\alpha = \frac{({{I}})({{w}})}{{{t}}} = {{answer}}\ \text{N}\cdot\text{m}


# ============================ 10.8 WORK AND POWER FOR ROTATIONAL MOTION ============================

## QUIZ 10.8
Q: A constant torque turns a wheel through two full turns. Which angle goes in \(W = \tau\Delta\theta\)?
A) \(4\pi\) rad
B) 2 rad
C) 720
D) \(2\pi\) rad
Answer: A
Why: Each turn is \(2\pi\) rad, so two turns are \(4\pi\) rad. The angle must be in radians.

## QUIZ 10.8
Q: An engine delivers a fixed power. When it turns more slowly, what happens to the torque it can give?
A) It goes up
B) It goes down
C) It stays the same
D) It drops to zero
Answer: A
Why: \(P = \tau\omega\). At the same power, a smaller \(\omega\) means a bigger \(\tau\). That is why low gears give strong turning.

## QUIZ 10.8
Q: A motor does 200 J of net work on a flywheel that starts from rest. What is the flywheel's rotational kinetic energy afterward?
A) 200 J
B) 100 J
C) 400 J
D) It depends on the flywheel's radius
Answer: A
Why: The work energy theorem: net work equals the change in kinetic energy. From rest, \(K = 200\ \text{J}\).

## DRILL 10.8
Q: A steady torque of \({{tau}}\ \text{N}\cdot\text{m}\) turns a winch through \({{n}}\) full turns. How much work does it do?
Let: tau = one of 5, 10, 20; n = one of 2, 3, 5, 10
Answer: tau*2*pi*n
Wrong: tau*n | Use radians: \(n\) turns is \(2\pi n\) rad.
Wrong: tau*360*n | Angles in \(W = \tau\Delta\theta\) must be in radians, not degrees.
Wrong: tau*pi*n | One turn is \(2\pi\) rad, not \(\pi\).
Unit: J
Work: W = \tau\Delta\theta = ({{tau}})(2\pi)({{n}}) = {{answer}}\ \text{J}

## DRILL 10.8
Q: A motor gives a torque of \({{tau}}\ \text{N}\cdot\text{m}\) while turning at \({{w}}\ \text{rad/s}\). What power does it deliver?
Let: tau = one of 5, 10, 20, 50; w = one of 10, 20, 50, 100
Answer: tau*w
Wrong: tau/w | Power is torque times angular velocity: \(P = \tau\omega\).
Wrong: 0.5*tau*w^2 | That mixes in the kinetic energy form. Power is \(P = \tau\omega\).
Wrong: tau*w*2*pi | \(\omega\) is already in rad/s; no extra \(2\pi\).
Unit: W
Work: P = \tau\omega = ({{tau}})({{w}}) = {{answer}}\ \text{W}

## DRILL 10.8
Q: A flywheel with moment of inertia \({{I}}\ \text{kg}\cdot\text{m}^2\) speeds up from rest to \({{w}}\ \text{rad/s}\). How much net work was done on it?
Let: I = one of 0.2, 0.5, 2, 4; w = one of 10, 20, 50
Answer: 0.5*I*w^2
Wrong: I*w^2 | Kinetic energy has a \(\tfrac12\): \(W = \tfrac12I\omega^2\).
Wrong: I*w | That is angular momentum, not work.
Wrong: 0.5*I*w | Square the angular velocity.
Unit: J
Work: W = \Delta K = \tfrac12I\omega^2 - 0 = \tfrac12({{I}})({{w}})^2 = {{answer}}\ \text{J}
