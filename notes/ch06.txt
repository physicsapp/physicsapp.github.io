## SUMMARY 6.1
In short: Every Newton's law problem follows the same steps: pick the object, draw its free body diagram, choose axes (one along the acceleration if you can), and write \(\sum F_x = ma_x\) and \(\sum F_y = ma_y\). For connected objects, draw a diagram for each one; a light rope over an ideal pulley has the same tension on both ends, and the objects share the same size of acceleration. In an elevator accelerating up, a scale reads \(m(g + a)\); accelerating down, \(m(g - a)\).
Watch out: The normal force and tension are whatever they need to be. Don't assume \(N = mg\) or \(T = mg\) when something is accelerating.

## SUMMARY 6.2
In short: Friction acts along a surface and opposes sliding or attempted sliding. Static friction holds a resting object in place and can be anything up to \(f_s = \mu_sN\). Once the object slides, kinetic friction is \(f_k = \mu_kN\), usually a bit smaller, which is why it's easier to keep a box moving than to start it. Both depend on the normal force, not on the contact area.
Watch out: \(\mu_sN\) is the largest possible static friction, not the actual value. If you push gently, static friction only matches your push.

## SUMMARY 6.3
In short: Any object moving in a circle needs a net force toward the center, the centripetal force \(F_c = mv^2/r\). It isn't a new kind of force: friction, tension, gravity, or the normal force supplies it. On a flat curve, static friction does the job, so the top speed is \(v = \sqrt{\mu_srg}\). On a curve banked at the ideal angle \(\tan\theta = v^2/(rg)\), the normal force alone turns the car.
Watch out: Never draw "centripetal force" as an extra arrow on a free body diagram. It's the net of the real forces.

## SUMMARY 6.4
In short: An object moving through air or water feels a drag force opposite its velocity. For everyday objects in air, \(F_D = \tfrac12C\rho Av^2\), so drag grows with the square of speed. A falling object speeds up until drag equals its weight; then the net force is zero and it falls at a steady terminal velocity. For tiny objects moving slowly in a thick fluid, drag follows Stokes' law, \(F_s = 6\pi r\eta v\), proportional to \(v\).
Watch out: At terminal velocity the object is still moving fast. It's the acceleration that is zero.

## EQUATIONS 6.2
* Static friction: \(f_s \le \mu_s N\) | \(\mu_s\) is the coefficient of static friction (no units) and \(N\) the normal force (N). Static friction is only as big as needed to stop sliding, up to a maximum of \(\mu_s N\).
* Kinetic friction: \(f_k = \mu_k N\) | \(\mu_k\) is the coefficient of kinetic friction (no units) and \(N\) the normal force (N). Once sliding, friction has this fixed size and points opposite to the sliding motion.

## EQUATIONS 6.3
* Centripetal force: \(F_c = \frac{mv^2}{r} = mr\omega^2\) | \(m\) is the mass (kg), \(v\) the speed (m/s), \(r\) the radius (m) and \(\omega\) the angular speed (rad/s). It is the net force toward the center needed for circular motion, supplied by real forces like tension, friction or gravity.
* Ideal angle of a banked curve: \(\tan\theta = \frac{v^2}{rg}\) | \(\theta\) is the bank angle, \(v\) the speed (m/s), \(r\) the radius of the curve (m) and \(g = 9.8\) m/s². At this angle the normal force alone supplies the centripetal force, so no friction is needed.

## EQUATIONS 6.4
* Drag force: \(F_D = \tfrac{1}{2}C\rho Av^2\) | \(C\) is the drag coefficient (no units), \(\rho\) the air density (kg/m³), \(A\) the front area (m²) and \(v\) the speed (m/s). Drag grows with the square of the speed, which is why falling objects reach a terminal velocity.
Stokes' law: \(F_s = 6\pi r\eta v\) | \(r\) is the radius of a small sphere (m), \(\eta\) the fluid's viscosity (Pa·s) and \(v\) the speed (m/s). For small, slow objects in a fluid, drag grows in proportion to the speed.

# ============================ 6.1 SOLVING PROBLEMS WITH NEWTON'S LAWS ============================

## QUIZ 6.1
Q: You stand on a scale in an elevator that is speeding up on its way up. How does the reading compare with your weight?
A) Greater
B) Less
C) Equal
D) Zero
Answer: A
Why: The floor has to push up harder than your weight to accelerate you upward: \(N = m(g + a)\).

## QUIZ 6.1
Q: An elevator moves down at constant speed. How does the scale reading compare with your weight?
A) Greater
B) Less
C) Equal
D) Zero
Answer: C
Why: Constant velocity means zero acceleration, so the normal force balances your weight, whichever way the elevator is moving.

## QUIZ 6.1
Q: The cable of an elevator snaps, and the elevator falls freely. What does a scale inside read?
A) Your weight
B) Twice your weight
C) Zero
D) Half your weight
Answer: C
Why: You and the scale both fall with \(a = g\), so the scale doesn't need to push on you: \(N = m(g - g) = 0\).

## QUIZ 6.1
Q: In an Atwood machine, two unequal masses hang from a rope over a light pulley. How does the tension compare with the weights?
A) It equals the heavier weight
B) It equals the lighter weight
C) It's between the two weights
D) It's the sum of the weights
Answer: C
Why: The heavier mass accelerates down, so \(T < m_1g\). The lighter one accelerates up, so \(T > m_2g\).

## QUIZ 6.1
Q: A rope pulls a box across the floor at an angle above horizontal. How does the normal force compare with the box's weight?
A) Greater
B) Less
C) Equal
D) Zero
Answer: B
Why: The rope's upward component helps hold the box up, so the floor pushes less: \(N = mg - T\sin\theta\).

# ============================ 6.2 FRICTION ============================

## QUIZ 6.2
Q: Why is it harder to start a heavy box sliding than to keep it sliding?
A) The box gets lighter once it moves
B) \(\mu_s\) is usually larger than \(\mu_k\)
C) Kinetic friction is zero
D) The normal force drops when it moves
Answer: B
Why: The maximum static friction \(\mu_sN\) is usually bigger than kinetic friction \(\mu_kN\), so you must push harder to break it loose.

## QUIZ 6.2
Q: A 10 kg box rests on a floor with \(\mu_s = 0.5\). You push it sideways with 20 N and it doesn't move. What is the friction force? (\(g = 10\ \text{m/s}^2\))
A) 50 N
B) 20 N
C) 0 N
D) 30 N
Answer: B
Why: Static friction only matches what's needed to keep the box still. 50 N is the most it could supply.

## QUIZ 6.2
Q: What does the friction force on a sliding block depend on?
A) The contact area
B) The normal force and the surfaces
C) The block's speed
D) The block's color
Answer: B
Why: In the simple model, \(f_k = \mu_kN\). The coefficient depends on the two surfaces, and the force doesn't depend on area or speed.

## QUIZ 6.2
Q: A block slides down a ramp at constant speed. What does that tell you about \(\mu_k\) and the angle \(\theta\)?
A) \(\mu_k = \sin\theta\)
B) \(\mu_k = \cos\theta\)
C) \(\mu_k = \tan\theta\)
D) \(\mu_k = 0\)
Answer: C
Why: Constant speed means friction balances the pull along the ramp: \(\mu_kmg\cos\theta = mg\sin\theta\), so \(\mu_k = \tan\theta\).

## QUIZ 6.2
Q: A crate sits in the back of a truck that speeds up. The crate doesn't slide. What force accelerates the crate?
A) Static friction, forward
B) Static friction, backward
C) Kinetic friction
D) No force is needed
Answer: A
Why: The crate speeds up with the truck, so it needs a forward net force. Static friction from the truck bed supplies it.

# ============================ 6.3 CENTRIPETAL FORCE ============================

## QUIZ 6.3
Q: A car rounds a flat curve at constant speed. What force provides the centripetal force?
A) The engine
B) Static friction between the tires and road
C) Kinetic friction
D) The car's weight
Answer: B
Why: The tires grip the road without sliding sideways, so static friction points toward the center of the curve.

## QUIZ 6.3
Q: A ball on a string is whirled in a horizontal circle, and the string breaks. Which way does the ball go?
A) Straight out from the center
B) Along the tangent to the circle at that point
C) Toward the center
D) It keeps circling
Answer: B
Why: With no net force, the ball keeps the velocity it had, which was tangent to the circle.

## QUIZ 6.3
Q: Passengers in a turning car feel pushed toward the outside of the curve. What's really happening?
A) A centrifugal force pushes them outward
B) Their bodies tend to keep going straight while the car turns
C) Gravity pulls them outward
D) The seat pushes them outward
Answer: B
Why: In the road's inertial frame, there's no outward force. The car turns under them, and the door or seat pushes them inward.

## QUIZ 6.3
Q: On a curve banked at the ideal angle for the car's speed, which force turns the car?
A) Friction
B) The horizontal component of the normal force
C) The car's weight
D) The engine
Answer: B
Why: The road tilts, so the normal force has a component toward the center. At the ideal speed, no friction is needed.

## QUIZ 6.3
Q: A car's speed on a flat curve doubles. How much more sideways friction does it need?
A) Twice as much
B) Four times as much
C) Half as much
D) The same
Answer: B
Why: \(F_c = mv^2/r\), so doubling \(v\) quadruples the needed force.

Let: v = 10 to 30 by 5; r = v^2/10
Answer: sqrt(r*10)
Wrong: r*10 | Take the square root: \(v^2 = rg\tan\theta\).
Wrong: sqrt(r) | Include \(g\).
Wrong: sqrt(r*10)/2 | At \(45^\circ\), \(\tan\theta = 1\), so \(v = \sqrt{rg}\).
Unit: m/s
Work: \tan\theta = \frac{v^2}{rg}\ \Rightarrow\ v = \sqrt{rg(1)} = \sqrt{({{r}})(10)} = {{answer}}\ \text{m/s}

## DRILL 6.3
Q: A \({{m}}\ \text{kg}\) ball swings on a string through the bottom of a vertical circle of radius \({{r}}\ \text{m}\) at \({{v}}\ \text{m/s}\). What is the tension at the bottom? Use \(g = 10\ \text{m/s}^2\).
Let: m = one of 0.5, 1, 2; r = one of 0.5, 1, 2; v = 2 to 4 by 1
Answer: m*10 + m*v^2/r
Wrong: m*v^2/r | At the bottom the string must also hold up the ball's weight.
Wrong: m*v^2/r - m*10 | At the bottom, tension points up and weight down. The tension is the larger one.
Wrong: m*10 | The ball is moving in a circle, so the tension must be larger than its weight.
Unit: N

# ============================ 6.4 DRAG AND TERMINAL SPEED ============================

## QUIZ 6.4
Q: A car doubles its speed on the highway. By what factor does the air drag grow?
A) 2
B) 4
C) 8
D) It stays the same
Answer: B
Why: \(F_D = \tfrac12C\rho Av^2\), so doubling \(v\) multiplies the drag by 4.

## QUIZ 6.4
Q: A skydiver falls at terminal velocity. What is the net force on her?
A) Her weight, downward
B) Zero
C) Upward
D) The drag force
Answer: B
Why: At terminal velocity, drag balances weight, so the net force and the acceleration are zero.

## QUIZ 6.4
Q: A skydiver opens her parachute. Why does her terminal velocity drop?
A) Her weight drops
B) The larger area \(A\) makes drag reach her weight at a lower speed
C) The air gets thicker
D) Gravity weakens
Answer: B
Why: Drag grows with area. With a big parachute, drag balances her weight at a much lower speed.

## QUIZ 6.4
Q: Just after a skydiver jumps from a hovering helicopter, what is her acceleration?
A) Zero
B) About \(g\), downward
C) Larger than \(g\)
D) Upward
Answer: B
Why: At first her speed is nearly zero, so drag is nearly zero and gravity is the only big force.

## QUIZ 6.4
Q: For a tiny bead sinking slowly in honey, how does the drag depend on speed?
A) It is proportional to \(v^2\)
B) It is proportional to \(v\)
C) It doesn't depend on speed
D) It is proportional to \(1/v\)
Answer: B
Why: Small, slow objects in a thick fluid follow Stokes' law, \(F_s = 6\pi r\eta v\), which is proportional to \(v\).

## DRILL 6.4
Q: For a cyclist, \(\tfrac12C\rho A = {{b}}\ \text{kg/m}\). What is the drag force at \({{v}}\ \text{m/s}\)?
Let: b = one of 0.1, 0.2, 0.25, 0.5; v = 4 to 12 by 2
Answer: b*v^2
Wrong: b*v | Drag grows with the square of the speed.
Wrong: 0.5*b*v^2 | The \(\tfrac12\) is already in \(b\).
Wrong: b*v^2*10 | There's no \(g\) in the drag force.
Unit: N
Work: F_D = \tfrac12C\rho Av^2 = ({{b}})({{v}})^2 = {{answer}}\ \text{N}

## DRILL 6.4
Q: A falling object has \(\tfrac12C\rho A = {{b}}\ \text{kg/m}\) and a weight of \({{w}}\ \text{N}\). What is its terminal speed?
Let: v = 10 to 50 by 10; b = one of 0.1, 0.2, 0.5; w = b*v^2
Answer: sqrt(w/b)
Wrong: w/b | Set \(bv^2 = w\), then take the square root.
Wrong: sqrt(w*b) | Divide by \(b\), don't multiply.
Wrong: sqrt(w/b)/2 | The \(\tfrac12\) is already in \(b\).
Unit: m/s
Work: bv_T^2 = w\ \Rightarrow\ v_T = \sqrt{\frac{{{w}}}{{{b}}}} = {{answer}}\ \text{m/s}

## DRILL 6.4
Q: An object's drag force is \({{F}}\ \text{N}\) at \({{v}}\ \text{m/s}\). What is the drag force at \({{n}}\) times that speed, with \(F_D \propto v^2\)?
Let: F = 10 to 50 by 10; v = 5 to 20 by 5; n = one of 2, 3
Answer: F*n^2
Wrong: F*n | Drag goes as \(v^2\), so multiply by \(n^2\).
Wrong: F*n^3 | Drag goes as \(v^2\), not \(v^3\).
Wrong: F | Drag depends on speed.
Unit: N
Work: F_D' = F_D\left(\frac{v'}{v}\right)^2 = ({{F}})({{n}})^2 = {{answer}}\ \text{N}

## DRILL 6.4
Q: A skydiver with a mass of \({{m}}\ \text{kg}\) falls at terminal velocity. How large is the drag force on her? Use \(g = 10\ \text{m/s}^2\).
Let: m = one of 50, 60, 70, 90
Answer: m*10
Wrong: 0 | She's moving fast through the air, so drag is large. It's the net force that is zero.
Wrong: m | That's her mass. Drag balances her weight, \(mg\).
Wrong: 2*m*10 | At terminal velocity, drag equals weight exactly.
Unit: N
Work: F_D = mg = ({{m}})(10) = {{answer}}\ \text{N}
