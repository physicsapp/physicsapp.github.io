## SUMMARY 2.1
In short: A scalar has size only, like mass or time. A vector has size and direction, like displacement or velocity, and is drawn as an arrow whose length is its magnitude. To add vectors on paper, place them tail to head; the resultant runs from the first tail to the last head, and the order doesn't matter. Multiplying a vector by a positive number stretches or shrinks it; a negative number also flips it around.
Watch out: Magnitudes don't simply add. Two vectors of 3 and 4 units can add up to anything from 1 to 7 units, depending on their directions.

## SUMMARY 2.2
In short: In a plane, a vector \(\vec A\) splits into components along the axes: \(\vec A = A_x\hat i + A_y\hat j\), with \(A_x = A\cos\theta_A\) and \(A_y = A\sin\theta_A\) when \(\theta_A\) is measured counterclockwise from the positive \(x\) axis. Going back, \(A = \sqrt{A_x^2 + A_y^2}\) and \(\tan\theta_A = A_y/A_x\). In three dimensions, add a \(z\) component and include \(A_z^2\) under the square root.
Watch out: A calculator's \(\tan^{-1}\) only gives angles between \(-90^\circ\) and \(90^\circ\). If \(A_x\) is negative, add \(180^\circ\).

## SUMMARY 2.3
In short: To add vectors without drawing, add their components: \(R_x = A_x + B_x + \dots\) and \(R_y = A_y + B_y + \dots\), then find the magnitude and direction of \(\vec R\) from its components. Subtracting \(\vec B\) means adding \(-\vec B\), which flips every component's sign. A unit vector \(\hat V = \vec V/V\) has magnitude 1 and points the same way as \(\vec V\).
Watch out: Add components, not magnitudes. \(|\vec A + \vec B|\) is not \(A + B\) unless the vectors point the same way.

## SUMMARY 2.4
In short: The scalar (dot) product \(\vec A\cdot\vec B = AB\cos\varphi = A_xB_x + A_yB_y + A_zB_z\) is a number. It is zero for perpendicular vectors and negative when the angle is more than \(90^\circ\). The vector (cross) product \(\vec A\times\vec B\) has magnitude \(AB\sin\varphi\) and points perpendicular to both vectors, by the right hand rule. Swapping the order flips its sign: \(\vec B\times\vec A = -\vec A\times\vec B\).
Watch out: Parallel vectors have a cross product of zero, and perpendicular vectors have a dot product of zero. Don't mix up which is which.

## EQUATIONS 2.1
Multiplying a vector by a scalar: \(\vec{B} = \alpha \vec{A}\)
Magnitude after multiplying by a scalar: \(B = |\alpha| A\)
Resultant of two displacements: \(\vec{D}_{AD} = \vec{D}_{AC} + \vec{D}_{CD}\)
Commutative law: \(\vec{A} + \vec{B} = \vec{B} + \vec{A}\)
Associative law: \((\vec{A} + \vec{B}) + \vec{C} = \vec{A} + (\vec{B} + \vec{C})\)
Distributive law: \(\alpha_1 \vec{A} + \alpha_2 \vec{A} = (\alpha_1 + \alpha_2)\vec{A}\)

## EQUATIONS 2.2
* Component form in two dimensions: \(\vec{A} = A_x \hat{i} + A_y \hat{j}\)
Components from the tail and head: \(A_x = x_e - x_b, \quad A_y = y_e - y_b\)
* Magnitude in a plane: \(A = \sqrt{A_x^2 + A_y^2}\)
* Direction angle in a plane: \(\theta_A = \tan^{-1}\!\left(\frac{A_y}{A_x}\right)\)
* Components from magnitude and angle: \(A_x = A \cos\theta_A, \quad A_y = A \sin\theta_A\)
Polar coordinates: \(x = r\cos\varphi, \quad y = r\sin\varphi\)
* Component form in three dimensions: \(\vec{A} = A_x \hat{i} + A_y \hat{j} + A_z \hat{k}\)
The z component: \(A_z = z_e - z_b\)
* Magnitude in three dimensions: \(A = \sqrt{A_x^2 + A_y^2 + A_z^2}\)

## EQUATIONS 2.3
Distributive property: \(\alpha(\vec{A} + \vec{B}) = \alpha \vec{A} + \alpha \vec{B}\)
Antiparallel vector: \(-\vec{A} = -A_x \hat{i} - A_y \hat{j} - A_z \hat{k}\)
Equal vectors: \(A_x = B_x, \quad A_y = B_y, \quad A_z = B_z\)
* Resultant of N vectors: \(\begin{aligned} F_{Rx} &= \textstyle\sum_{k=1}^{N} F_{kx} \\ F_{Ry} &= \textstyle\sum_{k=1}^{N} F_{ky} \\ F_{Rz} &= \textstyle\sum_{k=1}^{N} F_{kz} \end{aligned}\)
* Unit vector: \(\hat{V} = \frac{\vec{V}}{V}\)

## EQUATIONS 2.4
* Scalar (dot) product: \(\vec{A} \cdot \vec{B} = AB\cos\varphi\)
Commutative property of the dot product: \(\vec{A} \cdot \vec{B} = \vec{B} \cdot \vec{A}\)
Distributive property of the dot product: \(\vec{A} \cdot (\vec{B} + \vec{C}) = \vec{A} \cdot \vec{B} + \vec{A} \cdot \vec{C}\)
* Dot product from components: \(\vec{A} \cdot \vec{B} = A_x B_x + A_y B_y + A_z B_z\)
* Angle between two vectors: \(\cos\varphi = \frac{\vec{A} \cdot \vec{B}}{AB}\)
Dot products of unit vectors: \(\begin{aligned} \hat{i} \cdot \hat{i} = \hat{j} \cdot \hat{j} = \hat{k} \cdot \hat{k} &= 1 \\ \hat{i} \cdot \hat{j} = \hat{j} \cdot \hat{k} = \hat{k} \cdot \hat{i} &= 0 \end{aligned}\)
* Magnitude of the vector (cross) product: \(|\vec{A} \times \vec{B}| = AB\sin\varphi\)
Anticommutative property of the cross product: \(\vec{A} \times \vec{B} = -\vec{B} \times \vec{A}\)
Distributive property of the cross product: \(\vec{A} \times (\vec{B} + \vec{C}) = \vec{A} \times \vec{B} + \vec{A} \times \vec{C}\)
* Cross products of unit vectors: \(\hat{i} \times \hat{j} = \hat{k}, \quad \hat{j} \times \hat{k} = \hat{i}, \quad \hat{k} \times \hat{i} = \hat{j}\)
* Cross product from components: \(\begin{aligned} \vec{A} \times \vec{B} = {} & (A_y B_z - A_z B_y)\hat{i} \\ & + (A_z B_x - A_x B_z)\hat{j} \\ & + (A_x B_y - A_y B_x)\hat{k} \end{aligned}\)

# ============================ 2.1 SCALARS AND VECTORS ============================

## QUIZ 2.1
Q: Which of these is a vector?
A) Temperature
B) Mass
C) Velocity
D) Time
Answer: C
Why: Velocity has a magnitude and a direction. Temperature, mass, and time have size only.

## QUIZ 2.1
Q: Vectors \(\vec A\) and \(\vec B\) have magnitudes 3 and 4. Which magnitude is impossible for \(\vec A + \vec B\)?
A) 1
B) 5
C) 7
D) 8
Answer: D
Why: The resultant is largest, 7, when the vectors point the same way and smallest, 1, when they point opposite ways. 8 is out of reach.

## QUIZ 2.1
Q: What does multiplying \(\vec A\) by \(-2\) do?
A) Doubles its length and keeps its direction
B) Doubles its length and reverses its direction
C) Halves its length and reverses its direction
D) Makes its magnitude negative
Answer: B
Why: The magnitude becomes \(|-2|A = 2A\), and the minus sign reverses the direction. A magnitude is never negative.

## QUIZ 2.1
Q: When you add vectors tail to head, where does the resultant go?
A) From the tail of the first vector to the head of the last
B) From the head of the first vector to the tail of the last
C) Along the longest vector
D) From the origin to the head of the first vector
Answer: A
Why: The resultant connects where you started to where you ended, the first tail to the last head.

## QUIZ 2.1
Q: Two vectors are antiparallel. What does that mean?
A) They are perpendicular
B) They point in exactly opposite directions
C) They have the same magnitude
D) They point the same way
Answer: B
Why: Antiparallel vectors lie along the same line but point opposite ways, with direction angles \(180^\circ\) apart. Their magnitudes can differ.

## QUIZ 2.1
Q: Does the order of adding vectors change the resultant?
A) Yes, always
B) No, \(\vec A + \vec B = \vec B + \vec A\)
C) Only for three or more vectors
D) Only if they are perpendicular
Answer: B
Why: Vector addition is commutative. Drawing them in either order gives the same arrow from start to finish.

# ============================ 2.2 COMPONENTS ============================

## QUIZ 2.2
Q: A vector points into the second quadrant (up and to the left). What are the signs of its components?
A) \(A_x > 0,\ A_y > 0\)
B) \(A_x < 0,\ A_y > 0\)
C) \(A_x < 0,\ A_y < 0\)
D) \(A_x > 0,\ A_y < 0\)
Answer: B
Why: Left means negative \(x\), and up means positive \(y\).

## QUIZ 2.2
Q: A vector has components \(A_x = -3\) and \(A_y = -3\). What is its direction angle, measured counterclockwise from the positive \(x\) axis?
A) \(45^\circ\)
B) \(135^\circ\)
C) \(225^\circ\)
D) \(315^\circ\)
Answer: C
Why: Both components are negative, so the vector is in the third quadrant. \(\tan^{-1}(1) = 45^\circ\), and adding \(180^\circ\) gives \(225^\circ\).

## QUIZ 2.2
Q: Can a component of a vector be larger than the vector's magnitude?
A) Yes, if the angle is large
B) No, a component is at most the magnitude
C) Only in three dimensions
D) Only for negative components
Answer: B
Why: \(A = \sqrt{A_x^2 + A_y^2}\), so \(|A_x|\) can't exceed \(A\). They're equal only when the vector lies along the \(x\) axis.

## QUIZ 2.2
Q: In polar coordinates, a point is described by \(r\) and \(\varphi\). What is \(r\)?
A) The \(x\) coordinate
B) The distance from the origin
C) The angle from the \(y\) axis
D) The sum \(x + y\)
Answer: B
Why: \(r = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}\) is the distance from the origin, and \(\varphi\) is the angle from the positive \(x\) axis.

## QUIZ 2.2
Q: A vector of magnitude 10 points at \(\theta = 90^\circ\). What are its components?
A) \(A_x = 10,\ A_y = 0\)
B) \(A_x = 0,\ A_y = 10\)
C) \(A_x = 5,\ A_y = 5\)
D) \(A_x = 7.1,\ A_y = 7.1\)
Answer: B
Why: \(90^\circ\) is straight along the positive \(y\) axis: \(A_x = 10\cos 90^\circ = 0\) and \(A_y = 10\sin 90^\circ = 10\).

## QUIZ 2.3
Q: \(\vec A = 3\hat i + 2\hat j\). What is \(-\vec A\)?
A) \(-3\hat i + 2\hat j\)
B) \(3\hat i - 2\hat j\)
C) \(-3\hat i - 2\hat j\)
D) \(-5\)
Answer: C
Why: The antiparallel vector flips the sign of every component.

## QUIZ 2.3
Q: What is the magnitude of any unit vector?
A) 0
B) 1
C) It depends on the vector
D) \(\sqrt 2\)
Answer: B
Why: A unit vector is a vector divided by its own magnitude, \(\hat V = \vec V/V\), so its magnitude is exactly 1. It only carries direction.

## QUIZ 2.3
Q: Two vectors are equal. What must be true?
A) Their magnitudes are equal
B) All their components are equal
C) They start at the same point
D) Both A and B
Answer: D
Why: Equal vectors have the same magnitude and the same direction, which means every component matches. They can be drawn anywhere.

## QUIZ 2.3
Q: Why are component methods better than drawing arrows to scale?
A) Drawings always give the wrong direction
B) Components give exact results; drawings are only as good as your ruler and protractor
C) Components work only in two dimensions
D) There is no difference
Answer: B
Why: Analytical methods are exact. Graphical methods are approximate and get hard with many vectors or in three dimensions.

## QUIZ 2.3
Q: Three forces act on a knot that stays still. What is their vector sum?
A) The largest force
B) Zero
C) The sum of their magnitudes
D) It depends on the angles
Answer: B
Why: Forces that hold an object still balance out, so their resultant is the zero vector. Each component of the sum is zero.

# ============================ 2.4 PRODUCTS OF VECTORS ============================

## QUIZ 2.4
Q: Two nonzero vectors have a dot product of zero. What can you say?
A) They are parallel
B) They are perpendicular
C) They are antiparallel
D) One of them must be zero
Answer: B
Why: \(\vec A\cdot\vec B = AB\cos\varphi\), which is zero only when \(\cos\varphi = 0\), so \(\varphi = 90^\circ\).

## QUIZ 2.4
Q: Two nonzero vectors have a cross product of zero. What can you say?
A) They are perpendicular
B) They are parallel or antiparallel
C) They have equal magnitudes
D) Their dot product is also zero
Answer: B
Why: \(|\vec A\times\vec B| = AB\sin\varphi\), which is zero when \(\varphi = 0^\circ\) or \(180^\circ\).

## QUIZ 2.4
Q: What is \(\hat i\times\hat j\)?
A) \(\hat k\)
B) \(-\hat k\)
C) 1
D) 0
Answer: A
Why: The unit vectors follow the right hand rule in cyclic order: \(\hat i\times\hat j = \hat k\), \(\hat j\times\hat k = \hat i\), and \(\hat k\times\hat i = \hat j\).

## QUIZ 2.4
Q: What is \(\hat j\times\hat i\)?
A) \(\hat k\)
B) \(-\hat k\)
C) 1
D) 0
Answer: B
Why: Swapping the order of a cross product flips its sign, so \(\hat j\times\hat i = -\hat i\times\hat j = -\hat k\).

## QUIZ 2.4
Q: What kind of quantity does a dot product give?
A) A vector perpendicular to both
B) A scalar
C) A unit vector
D) A vector along the first one
Answer: B
Why: The dot product is a number, which is why it's also called the scalar product. Work is an example.

## QUIZ 2.4
Q: The angle between two vectors is \(120^\circ\). What is the sign of their dot product?
A) Positive
B) Negative
C) Zero
D) It depends on the magnitudes
Answer: B
Why: \(\cos 120^\circ = -0.5\), so the dot product is negative whenever the angle is more than \(90^\circ\).
